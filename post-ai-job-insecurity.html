The layoff hasn't happened.
Your body is already bracing.

On AI & identity · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

Nobody has said anything to you. Your reviews are still good. Your manager still forwards you the hard problems. And yet, most evenings now, there is a low hum underneath the day that was not there two years ago. You read another headline about a team being replaced by a model. You watched a tool do, in nine seconds, a version of a thing that used to be a morning of your work. And some quiet part of you started keeping score.

Here is the part that is worth saying plainly, because almost nobody says it to the people carrying it: you do not need to actually lose your job for this to be costing you. The bracing is the cost. The research now supports what your body already worked out on its own.

Can job insecurity hurt you before any layoff happens?

Yes, and this is the finding that should change how you think about the whole thing. In 2025, a study published in the Journal of Innovation & Knowledge followed 403 workers over time and asked a precise question: when a company brings in AI, what actually happens to employee mental health, and through what path. The answer was not the one most people assume. AI adoption, on its own, showed no direct link to depression. The harm appeared only through one channel: job insecurity, the anticipatory fear of losing your place.

Read that carefully, because it relocates the whole problem. The damage was not in the technology. It was in the uncertainty the technology created. The tool on your screen is not what keeps you awake at one in the morning. The unanswered question about what it means for you is.

The layoff is a single day. The insecurity is every day before it.

How common is this fear now, really?

Common enough that feeling it is closer to normal than to unusual. In ADP Research's Today at Work 2026 report, drawn from a Global Workforce Survey of more than 39,000 workers across 36 markets, only 22% strongly agreed that their job was safe from being eliminated. Fewer than one in four. That means roughly three in four workers are carrying some live version of the exact question you have been carrying, most of them in silence, most of them assuming they are the anxious exception rather than the rule.

If it helps to hear it directly: the feeling you have been quietly judging yourself for is, on the current numbers, the majority experience. You are not soft. You are early, or you are honest, and the rest of the room is having the same private conversation with the ceiling at night.

Why does this land harder on senior professionals?

You would think experience would be armour. Often it is the opposite. The further you have gone on one path, the more of yourself you have invested in it, and the more a threat to the role reads as a threat to the person. A junior worker who fears automation is worried about a job. A director who has spent twenty years becoming the person who is good at this specific thing is, underneath the job worry, quietly asking who they are if the thing stops mattering.

In September 2025, two University of Florida researchers gave this its clinical name. In a paper in Cureus, Stephanie McNamara and Dr. Joseph Thornton proposed AI Replacement Dysfunction, a framework for the distress of facing professional obsolescence. The symptoms they list are not vague unease. They include anxiety, insomnia, loss of identity, feelings of worthlessness and hopelessness. Thornton described the phenomenon in a single line that lands because it is true: AI displacement, he said, is an invisible disaster. Invisible because no ambulance comes for a threat that has not happened yet, and because the person suffering it usually looks, from the outside, entirely fine.

What the fear does to your actual work

There is a cruel loop hidden in all of this. The mental bandwidth that anticipatory insecurity eats is the same bandwidth your best work runs on. Worry is not free. It runs quietly in the background, taking up the exact working memory and focus you would otherwise spend being good at the job you are afraid of losing. So the fear of becoming less capable can, if left running long enough, make you genuinely less capable, which feeds the fear, which takes more bandwidth. The story you are dreading starts, in small ways, to help write itself.

This is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to treat the worry as a real load with a real cost, rather than a background noise you are supposed to just tolerate. What actually helps

The instinct, when the fear is this large and this shapeless, is to look away from it. That is exactly backwards. A fear that has no edges will expand to fill whatever room you give it, which is why the dread so often feels bigger than any single fact could justify. The first real relief comes from giving it edges.

Start by turning the vague verdict into a specific picture. The fear tends to speak in totals, as if all of your work were equally exposed. It almost never is. Some of what you do is genuinely automatable, and some of it, usually the judgment, the relationships, the reading of a room, is not, and the two get blurred into one undifferentiated threat. For a lot of people, that first honest map is the moment the noise drops.

The second move is the one no tool can do for you, because it is not a numbers question. Underneath the practical fear of losing income sits a quieter question about identity: who are you if the work that has defined you changes shape. That question is genuinely hard to hold alone, not because you lack the intelligence for it, but because you are too close to your own life to see which parts of your worth were ever really tied to the job title in the first place. That is the part a real conversation is for. Not reassurance that everything will be fine, and not career advice about which skill to reskill into next. A second, steadier vantage point on a fear that, from the inside, has no edges.

There is also a genuinely hopeful reading buried in the same research that named the problem. If the harm runs through insecurity rather than through the technology itself, then insecurity, not the march of AI, is the thing you get to work on. The same 2025 study found that the link between AI and that fear was weaker where people felt supported and had some sense of control. You cannot slow down the technology. You can absolutely change how alone you face it, and how clearly you see your own actual position inside it. On the evidence, that is not a consolation prize. It is the lever.

Common questions about AI job insecurity and mental health

Can job insecurity affect your mental health before any layoff happens?

Yes. The distress does not wait for a termination letter. A 2025 longitudinal study of 403 workers, published in the Journal of Innovation & Knowledge, found that AI adoption did not raise depression directly. Its effect on mental health ran through job insecurity, which is the anticipatory fear of losing your role. That fear is present tense. It does its work while your job is still, on paper, perfectly safe.

Is it the AI itself that harms mental health, or the fear of it?

The research points at the fear. In the 2025 Journal of Innovation & Knowledge study, the technology on its own showed no direct link to depression. The harm appeared only through the pathway of job insecurity. Practically, that means the tool on your screen is not what keeps you awake. The unanswered question about what it means for you is. That distinction matters, because a feeling built on an unanswered question can be worked with.

Why does AI job insecurity hit senior professionals so hard?

Because senior professionals have more identity, not less, tied up in the role. When a title and a form of expertise are how you have understood yourself for twenty years, the threat of obsolescence reads as a threat to who you are, not just to your income. UF researchers named the clinical version of this in 2025 as AI Replacement Dysfunction, with symptoms including loss of identity, worthlessness and hopelessness. The higher you have climbed on one ladder, the more it costs to imagine the ladder disappearing.

What actually helps with anticipatory AI job anxiety?

Two moves, in order. First, convert the vague dread into a specific picture. Clearhead's free AI Automation & Relevance Index scores which parts of your work are genuinely exposed and which are not, so the fear stops speaking for the whole of you. Second, talk the identity question through with someone outside your own head, because the part of this that a spreadsheet cannot reach is the quiet question of who you are if the work changes. Naming the real size of the threat is most of the relief.
