You give more than you get back.
Your mind keeps the receipt.

On effort & reward · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

You are not lazy. You are not fragile. You put in more than almost anyone around you, and you have done so for years. So the tiredness that has settled into you lately does not fit the story you tell about yourself, which is why it is so confusing. You keep waiting to feel proud of what you carry, and instead you mostly feel spent.

Here is the part that is hard to say out loud. It is not the amount of work. It is the exchange rate. Somewhere along the way, the effort you pour in stopped coming back to you as anything you can feel. Not as recognition, not as security, not as a sense that you are moving somewhere. And there is now a large body of research showing that this specific gap, giving a lot and getting little back, is one of the most reliable predictors of work stress turning into something heavier.

If you take only one thing from this page, take this: when effort you put in at work is not matched by the reward you get back, meaning pay, respect, security, and progress, your risk of depression rises measurably. It is called effort-reward imbalance, and after three decades of study it is one of the best-evidenced ways that work quietly wears down a mind. The problem was never that you feel too much. The problem is real, and it has a name.

What is effort-reward imbalance, and why does it matter?

The idea comes from the sociologist Johannes Siegrist, who noticed something that pay slips miss. Work is not just a transaction of hours for money. It is a deeper contract of effort for reward, where reward means three things at once: the money, yes, but also the esteem you are shown, and the sense of security and advancement that tells you the ground under you is solid. When effort keeps climbing and those rewards do not follow, the body reads it as a contract being broken, over and over, every day.

In February 2026, Siegrist published a review of three decades of research into this model in the Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment and Health. His conclusion, drawn from many studies across many countries, was blunt in its own careful way: exposure to effort-reward imbalance is consistently tied to a moderately elevated risk of both heart disease and depression. This is not a mood or a motivational problem. It is a measured occupational health risk, sitting quietly inside jobs that look, from the outside, like success.

Burnout is not always about how much you do. Sometimes it is about how little of it ever comes back to you.

What does the research actually say?

The single clearest number comes from a 2017 meta-analysis by Reiner Rugulies and colleagues, also in the Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment and Health. They pooled eight prospective cohort studies, following 84,963 employees over time who did not have depression at the start. Of those, 2,897 went on to develop a depressive disorder. Workers exposed to effort-reward imbalance had roughly 65% higher risk of developing depression than those who were not exposed.

Sit with the shape of that finding for a moment. These were people tracked forward in time, healthy at the outset, sorted only by whether their work asked more of them than it gave back. The ones stuck on the wrong side of that exchange were markedly more likely to become unwell later. The imbalance came first. The depression followed. That order matters, because it means the feeling is not evidence that something is wrong with you. It is closer to evidence that something is wrong with the arrangement.

Why does the imbalance hurt more the longer it lasts?

Most people assume a bad year is survivable and then things reset. The research suggests it is not quite that forgiving. A 2025 longitudinal study of 3,104 middle-aged employees in Germany, published in Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology, did something most studies do not. It measured effort-reward imbalance twice, years apart, rather than once. What it found is that repeated exposure to the imbalance over time was linked to a greater risk of depression in late midlife than a single stressful episode was.

In plain terms, this does not average out. It accumulates. Each year spent giving more than you get back does not simply pass. It leaves a residue, and the residue stacks. This is part of why a twenty-year career can feel heavier than an anxious first job ever did, even when the first job paid less and offered nothing. You have been running the deficit longer, and some part of you has been keeping count the whole time, even when your calendar and your title said everything was fine.

Why senior people feel this most

There is a cruel geometry to seniority here. As you rise, the effort side of the equation almost never shrinks. The hours hold or grow. The emotional labour of holding a team, absorbing other people's anxiety, and being the one who stays calm becomes a second, invisible job layered on the first. But the reward side has a ceiling that arrives sooner than anyone warns you about. Past a certain level, another increment of pay stops feeling like anything. Recognition thins out, because you are now the person expected to be excellent rather than praised for it. Progress slows, because there are fewer rungs left above you.

So the gap widens precisely for the people who look, on paper, like they have won. You can be senior, well paid, and respected in the abstract, and still be running a daily deficit between what you give and what genuinely lands back. In more than 3,000 conversations with professionals, this is one of the most common quiet injuries I see, and one of the least spoken about, because who would you even complain to. The socially permitted response to your position is gratitude, so the depletion goes underground, where it does its work unwatched.

What actually helps

The unhelpful advice is to leave, or to care less. Most people cannot do the first and should not have to do the second. The more honest starting point is to stop treating the tiredness as a character flaw and start treating it as information about a real imbalance, then look at that imbalance directly instead of around it.

Two things tend to be tangled together and are worth pulling apart. The first is load: how much you are actually carrying, including the invisible parts that never show up in a job description. And then there is the part no tool can do. Seeing your own imbalance clearly is genuinely hard from inside it, because you have spent years normalising it in order to keep going, and the mind protects what it depends on. That is what an outside conversation is actually for. Not advice to quit, and not reassurance that you are fine. Sometimes naming the exchange rate out loud, to someone who is not inside your organisation, is the first honest accounting you have done in years.

Common questions about effort, reward, and work stress

What is effort-reward imbalance at work?

Effort-reward imbalance is a well-studied model of work stress that describes what happens when the effort you put into a job stays high while the rewards you get back, meaning pay, recognition, security, and career progress, stay low. The imbalance, not the workload alone, is what the research links to poorer mental health. You can be busy and fine. It is giving a lot and getting little back that quietly does the damage.

Can giving more than you get back really cause depression?

Long-term studies point that way. A 2017 meta-analysis of eight prospective cohort studies covering 84,963 employees found that workers exposed to effort-reward imbalance had roughly 65% higher risk of a later depressive disorder than workers who were not. A 2026 review of three decades of this research reached the same broad conclusion: the imbalance is consistently tied to a moderately raised risk of depression. It is a risk factor, not a diagnosis, but it is a real and measured one.

Why do I feel worse now than when I had less responsibility?

Partly because seniority quietly widens the gap. The effort side of the equation, the hours, the emotional labour, the being available, tends to climb steadily as you rise. The reward side, the sense of being genuinely recognised and progressing, often flattens once you are already senior and well paid. A 2025 German longitudinal study found that repeated exposure to this imbalance over time carried a greater risk of depression than a single stressful stretch, which is why an accumulated career can feel heavier than an early one.

What can I do if I cannot just leave the job?

Most people cannot, and leaving is rarely the first move anyway. The more useful first step is to see the imbalance clearly instead of blaming yourself for feeling worn down.
