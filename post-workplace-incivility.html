The meeting ended hours ago.
Your mind is still in it.

On everyday disrespect at work & the sleep it steals · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

It was nothing, really. You were mid-sentence and someone talked straight over you, and the room moved on as if you had not spoken. Or your manager read your message, you watched the little tick turn, and no reply came. Or the credit for your idea landed on someone else's name and nobody corrected it. Any one of these is far too small to raise. So you let it go, or you think you do. Then it is one in the morning and you are still in that room, running the sentence you should have said, wide awake over something you already told yourself did not matter.

Here is the part worth saying plainly. Those small slights have a name, workplace incivility, and there is now good evidence for what they cost. The rudeness takes a second. The replaying it sets off can run for hours, and that rumination is what breaks the sleep and follows you home to the people you love. What keeps you up is not weakness or oversensitivity. It is your mind doing unpaid overtime on a wound too small to show anyone.

What is workplace incivility, and why does something so small stick?

Researchers define incivility as low-intensity behaviour with an ambiguous intent to harm. That ambiguity is the whole problem. A colleague who is openly cruel gives you something clear to name. Incivility gives you a tone, a silence, a pointed omission, each deniable, each easy to wave off as you being touchy. So you do not complain, because there is nothing solid to point at, and you do not process it either, because on paper nothing happened. It goes underground instead, and the mind keeps turning it over precisely because it was never resolved out loud.

The other reason it sticks is that incivility reads as information about your standing. Being talked over, skipped, or dismissed is not just annoying. Some quieter part of you files it as a signal about where you sit, whether you are respected, whether you belong here. That is a heavier thing to carry than a single rude moment, and it is why an interaction that lasted five seconds can occupy your head for the rest of the night.

What does the latest research actually show?

In 2025, Hassan Imam and colleagues published a study in the Future Business Journal that followed 750 employees with daily questionnaires over two continuous weeks, catching the effect of everyday rudeness close to when it happened rather than from memory. The finding that matters most here is where the harm travelled. Incivility from a supervisor did not stay at work. It raised work-family conflict, the collision between what your job takes out of you and what your home life needs from you.

The study also traced part of the reason, and it is uncomfortable. One route by which a manager's rudeness reached the family table was a spillover of mistreatment, where the person on the receiving end passed the tension on to co-workers and customers during the day. The disrespect flows downhill and outward, so a single hostile source can sour a whole chain of interactions, and the version of you that this kind of day produces is not always the one you would choose to bring home. Naming that is not about blame. It is about seeing the current you are standing in.

Why does a five-second slight cost you a night of sleep?

For the mechanism, look at an earlier and closely relevant study. In 2019, Caitlin Demsky and colleagues published work in the Journal of Occupational Health Psychology that examined 699 employees and asked a precise question, how does rudeness at work reach your sleep. The answer they found was negative work rumination, the involuntary replaying of the incident during your own hours. Incivility was linked to more of this after-hours rumination, and that rumination, in turn, was linked to more symptoms of insomnia.

Read the chain slowly, because it is the important part. The rudeness is not what wakes you at two in the morning. The rudeness ended at four in the afternoon. What wakes you is your own mind still working on it, long after everyone else has forgotten it happened. You are not losing sleep to the event. You are losing it to the rehearsal, and the rehearsal is running because the day gave you nowhere to put the thing down.

The rude remark takes a second. The replaying of it takes the night. Most people blame themselves for the sleeplessness, when the real cost was the rumination they were never given a place to set down.

Why does this hit senior and conscientious people hardest?

You might expect that experience would armour you against small slights. Often it does the opposite. The more you care about the work and the more responsible you are for the room, the more information you read into a dismissive tone, and the more of it you absorb without passing it on, because passing it on is beneath the standard you hold yourself to. So you take the hit and swallow the response, and the swallowed response is exactly the material rumination feeds on all night.

This is the same undertow that runs through a lot of quiet professional distress. It sits close to the low hum of after-hours messages that never lets the day fully close, and to the sheer difficulty of switching off from work at all. Left running for months, this kind of unresolved load is part of what wears the body down through slow, accumulated stress. The single rude moment is not the danger. The danger is a hundred of them with no exit, quietly compounding.

Is there anything that actually protects you from it?

The Demsky study offers the most useful finding of all, and it is a hopeful one. The link between incivility and lost sleep was weakest for the people who had strong recovery experiences during their own time, meaning they could genuinely detach from work and relax rather than keep re-litigating the day in their heads. Recovery did not make them thicker-skinned. It gave the rumination somewhere to stop.

That points at what helps, and it is not the usual advice to simply let it go, which mostly adds a second job of policing your own feelings. What helps is making the load visible instead of vague, and then having a real place to set it down. Where a real conversation comes in

Notice what the research is really pointing at. Not a flaw in you, and not thinner skin to develop. A working life that hands you small disrespects with no clean way to name them, and a quiet belief that being kept awake by them is just proof you are too sensitive. That belief is hard to question alone, partly because the same conscientiousness that makes you a good colleague is very good at turning the blame inward.

What tends to help is not another tip. It is a real, two-way conversation with someone whose only job in that hour is to listen and help you think, a space where you do not have to decide whether the slight was big enough to mind, so the vague, sleepless churn can become a clear picture of what is actually happening and what you want to do about it. That is a fair description of what good coaching and counselling are for. For many people it is the first place in a long time where they can say the small thing out loud without being told it does not matter.

If you read this and recognised the two-in-the-morning rehearsal, the day that will not close, that is worth taking seriously, and worth talking through with a person rather than lying awake solving it alone for another night.

Common questions about rudeness at work and lost sleep

What is workplace incivility?

Workplace incivility is low-intensity rude or disrespectful behaviour with an unclear intent to harm, such as being talked over in a meeting, having your email pointedly ignored, a dismissive tone from a manager, or being left off a thread you should have been on. Each act is small enough to seem petty to complain about, which is exactly why it accumulates. Researchers separate it from outright bullying or harassment precisely because it is quieter and easier to wave away.

Why does a small slight at work keep me awake at night?

Because the mind keeps working on it after you have left. A 2019 study of 699 workers in the Journal of Occupational Health Psychology found that workplace incivility was linked to insomnia symptoms through negative work rumination, the replaying of the incident in your head during your own time. The slight itself takes a second. The rumination it sets off can run for hours, and that is what disturbs the sleep.

Can rudeness at work affect my family life?

The evidence suggests it can. A 2025 study of 750 employees in the Future Business Journal found that incivility from a supervisor raised work-family conflict, and that part of the reason was a spillover of the mistreatment, where the person on the receiving end passed the tension on to co-workers and customers. The version of you that a hostile workday produces does not always stay at the office. It often walks through your front door.

How do I recover from a rude or disrespectful workday?

The same 2019 study found that the link between incivility and lost sleep was weakest for people who had strong recovery experiences during their own time, meaning they could genuinely detach from work and relax rather than keep re-litigating the day. Recovery is not about pretending it did not happen. It is about having somewhere real to set it down, whether that is a boundary around after-hours contact, a conversation with someone who listens, or naming the load instead of carrying it silently.
