It isn't the fear of losing your job.
It's the fear that you're no longer steering.

On AI, work & identity · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

You are good at your job. On paper nothing is wrong. And yet somewhere in the last year a quiet unease moved in and never quite left. It is not that you think you will be fired next month. It is that you have stopped being sure where any of this is going. The skills that made you valuable feel like they have a shelf life now. The ladder you were climbing keeps changing shape under your feet. You cannot point to the threat, which is exactly why it is so hard to put down.

A study published this year gives that feeling a name and, more usefully, a mechanism. In February 2026, researchers writing in the Journal of Business and Psychology found that career insecurity, the sense that you are losing control of where your career is headed, harms mental health and pulls people back from their work, and that it does most of its damage through one specific thing: the feeling that you have lost your grip on your own path.

What is career insecurity, and why does it cut deeper than a single layoff fear?

The team, led by Anguo Fu with colleagues Shuaihua Wang, Tengfei Guo, Zhaoyang Xin, Shenyang Hai, Yufang Han and Xuyan Zhang, opens with a plain observation. The rapid advance of artificial intelligence, arriving on top of a shaky global economy, has made the labour market feel less predictable, and that has made career insecurity a common condition rather than a rare one. The key word is career, not job. Job insecurity is the fear of losing this job. Career insecurity is wider and longer. It is the fear that the whole road ahead has gone uncertain, that you no longer know what you are building toward or whether it will still exist when you get there.

That distinction matters because a lot of the people carrying this are not, in fact, about to be let go. This site already has a companion piece on why your body braces even when the layoff never comes. What the 2026 study adds is that a secure job does not settle a mind that has quietly lost faith in its own direction. You can have the salary and the title and still feel the ground moving.

So what actually does the damage? The loss of the wheel.

The researchers built their work on the transactional model of stress, the idea that a stressor harms us not by its raw size but by how we appraise our ability to cope with it. Using three waves of data collected over time from two very different worlds of work, emerging gig work and traditional service work, they traced a clear chain. Career insecurity raised what they call perceived control loss, the felt sense that you no longer have a hand on your own trajectory. And it was that loss of control, not the insecurity on its own, that then increased job withdrawal, the slow pulling back and going through the motions, and impaired mental health.

Read that order carefully, because it changes what the problem actually is. The thing eating at you is not really the headline about AI, or the reorg, or the market. It is the helplessness those things leave behind, the sense that events are deciding your future and you are only reacting. That is the same story a separate study on this site tells from another angle, that what wears people down is not the workload but how little they get to decide. Control, it turns out, is not a nice-to-have. It is load-bearing.

Career insecurity does not break you by threatening your job. It breaks you by convincing you that your hands are off the wheel, and a capable person who believes they cannot steer will quietly stop trying to.

Isn't everyone a bit anxious about work right now?

They are, and that is precisely why this is worth naming rather than normalising. The American Psychological Association's 2025 Work in America survey found that 54 percent of U.S. workers said job insecurity had a significant impact on their stress at work, with many worried about losing their role to policy and economic shifts. In India the picture is sharper still. A 2026 LinkedIn survey reported that around 84 percent of professionals felt unprepared to find a new job, and an analysis by Astrotalk found career-related anxiety climbed roughly 50 percent through 2025, with "is AI going to take my job?" becoming one of the most common questions people asked. When almost everyone around you feels the same drift, it starts to look like the weather instead of a wound. But a shared condition is still a condition, and it is still costing you something real.

Why do capable, senior people feel this the worst?

Because they have the most trajectory to lose. In the conversations I have had over the years, more than three thousand of them with professionals from Google, Meta, Apple and Fortune 500 teams, the people most shaken by the AI years are rarely the ones with the weakest position. They are the ones who spent fifteen or twenty years becoming excellent at something and can now feel the ground under that excellence shifting. Their fear is not "will I find work". It is "will the thing I am good at still matter, and if it does not, who am I". That is closer to the piece here on why the real fear is not losing the job but losing who you are, and it explains why reassurance about job security so rarely lands. You are not asking whether you will be employed. You are asking whether you are still the author of your own story.

What actually helps, when you cannot make the market certain?

Here is the part of the 2026 study that should change how you respond to all of this. The researchers did not stop at the damage. They tested what softened it, and found a clear buffer: supervisor career support mentoring. Where people had someone in their corner who helped them think about their path, the felt loss of control went down, and with it the harm to mental health and the pull toward withdrawal. The insecurity did not vanish. What changed was the helplessness. Support that helps you appraise your situation and see your own options quietly hands you back the wheel.

Notice what that is and is not. It is not a pep talk, and it is not a promise that AI will spare your role. It is a real relationship in which someone helps you convert a vague, swirling dread into a clear-eyed look at where you actually stand and what you can actually move. Where a real conversation comes in

A tool can show you the map. It cannot sit with you while you decide what to do with it. The 2026 study is, in the end, a finding about relationships: the antidote to feeling you have lost control was another person who helped you find it again. Most senior professionals do not lack information about their careers. They lack a space to think out loud with someone whose only job in that hour is to help them see clearly, without an agenda about what they should choose.

That is a fair description of what good coaching and counselling are for. Not advice, and not reassurance, but a steady, two-way conversation that turns the fog of "everything feels uncertain" into "here is where I stand, here is what I can influence, and here is the next honest step". You cannot make the years ahead certain. You can stop facing them as though you were only a passenger.

If you have been carrying a low, nameless unease about where your career is going, that is worth taking seriously, and it is worth talking through with a person rather than pushing down for another year.

Common questions about career insecurity and mental health

What is career insecurity, and how is it different from job insecurity?

Job insecurity is the fear of losing this particular job. Career insecurity is broader and longer, the fear that you are losing control of where your whole career is going. A 2026 study in the Journal of Business and Psychology frames career insecurity as a rising problem driven by AI and economic uncertainty, and shows it is prevalent even among people whose current job is safe. It hurts because it attacks not one paycheck but your sense of direction.

How does career insecurity affect mental health?

The 2026 study used three-wave data from gig workers and traditional service workers and found that career insecurity harms mental health mainly through a middle step called perceived control loss. When you feel career insecurity, you start to feel you have lost your grip on your own path, and it is that loss of control that then raises job withdrawal and damages mental health. The insecurity itself is less the wound than the helplessness it creates.

What actually helps career insecurity, if you cannot control the market?

The same 2026 study found that supportive mentoring buffers the damage. Supervisor career support mentoring reduced the felt loss of control, which softened the effect of career insecurity on both withdrawal and mental health. You cannot make the labour market certain, but a person who helps you think, weigh options and see your own agency can restore the sense of steering, and that is what protects you.

Is career anxiety about AI common right now?

Yes, and it is rising. The American Psychological Association's 2025 Work in America survey found 54 percent of U.S. workers said job insecurity had a significant impact on their stress at work. In India a 2026 LinkedIn survey reported that about 84 percent of professionals felt unprepared to find a new job, and an Astrotalk analysis found career-related anxiety rose roughly 50 percent in 2025 as AI worries grew. Feeling this does not mean you are fragile, it means you are paying attention.
