You are not tired from the work.
You are tired from the face you wear while doing it.

On emotional labour & masking · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

You are good at the face. The steady voice in the review when the number missed. The warm hello to the person you cannot stand. The measured nod in the meeting while something in you is quietly screaming. By the time you get home you are wrung out, and you cannot always point to why, because on paper the day was not even that heavy. The meetings were ordinary. The work got done. So why does it feel like you ran a marathon in a suit that does not fit.

Here is the part worth saying plainly. There is a name for what you have been doing, and there is now solid evidence for what it costs. Faking a feeling you do not have, all day, is called surface acting, and a 2025 study of 1,506 workers found it is one of the clearest routes into burnout and low wellbeing. The exhaustion you cannot explain is not weakness. It is the bill for the gap between your face and your feeling, and your mind has been paying it in a currency you do not track.

What is surface acting, and why does it cost more than the feeling itself?

Psychologists split the emotional side of work into two moves. One is deep acting, where you actually try to feel the thing you need to show, so you talk yourself into real patience with a difficult client and the warmth on your face is at least partly true. The other is surface acting, where you leave the inside alone and just manage the outside. You hold the smile, soften the tone, arrange the expression, while whatever you really feel sits underneath, unchanged and unexpressed.

The reason surface acting wears you down is not the smiling. It is the split. Holding a face that does not match what is going on inside takes constant, low-grade effort, the way holding your stomach in for an hour does. You stop noticing you are doing it, but the muscle never gets to rest. Do that from your first call to your last, five days a week, and the fatigue does not come from the tasks. It comes from the management of yourself around the tasks.

What does the latest research actually show?

In August 2025, Mårten Eriksson at the University of Gävle published a study in Frontiers in Organizational Psychology that surveyed 1,506 people in roles built on constant human contact, in healthcare, care and customer-facing service. Using a statistical model that traces how one thing feeds the next, the study followed a clear chain. Heavy emotional demands pushed people into surface acting. Surface acting drove burnout, measured as both exhaustion and disengagement. And that burnout, in turn, pulled their mental wellbeing down.

Two details matter for anyone reading this from a corner office rather than a care ward. First, the pattern held across every professional group in the study, which tells you this is about the emotional labour itself, not the specific job. Second, it held across every age, so it is not something you grow out of with seniority. If anything, the more your role runs on managing other people's feelings, the more of this you are quietly doing.

But isn't this just what low, anxious people do more of?

That is the fair objection, and one study cannot fully answer it, because it took its measurements at a single point in time. So look at an older piece of work that measured the same thing across two years. In 2020, Chunhui Suh and Laura Punnett published a prospective study in the International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health that followed care workers over time. They measured surface acting at the start, and then checked, two years later, who had gone on to develop depressive symptoms.

The people high in surface acting at the beginning were more likely to be showing depressive symptoms at the end. Because the faking was measured first and the low mood came after, the study points the arrow in the direction that is easy to miss. It is not only that flat, low people mask more. It is that the masking itself, held long enough, helps build the flatness. The face you keep putting on is not neutral. Over time it leaves a mark on the person behind it.

Surface acting does not feel like a mental health risk while you are doing it. It feels like being good at your job. That is exactly why it runs for years before anyone, including you, calls it what it is.

Why does this hit senior people hardest?

The studies looked at care and service workers, but the mechanism belongs to any role where part of the job is managing a face, and few roles demand more of that than leadership. The higher you go, the more your job becomes emotional weather for everyone below you. You absorb the bad news and pass down the calm version. You project confidence in the plan you privately have doubts about. You cannot be visibly rattled, because the room reads you and reacts. So you surface act, more and more, in front of more and more people, with fewer and fewer places to drop it.

This is the same undertow that runs through a lot of senior distress. It is there in the particular loneliness of being at the top, where the higher you rise the fewer people you can be unguarded with. It is there in the effort of being the one who always holds it together, and in the private gap between the competent face and the uncertain feeling. Surface acting is the daily machinery underneath all three, the small, constant, invisible work of keeping the outside steady while the inside does whatever it is actually doing.

How do you tell surface acting from healthy composure?

Not all composure is costly. When the calm you are showing lines up with something you genuinely feel, real care for a colleague, real interest in the problem, real steadiness because you have been here before, it costs you very little and can even feel good. The expensive version is the one where the face and the feeling have completely parted ways, and you hold that gap for hours with nowhere to put down what you are carrying.

The tell is in the aftermath. Honest, engaged work leaves you tired the way a good walk does, spent but settled. Sustained surface acting leaves you hollow, oddly numb, reaching for the phone or the drink or the silence just to stop performing for a while. If you get home and cannot locate a single feeling that is actually yours, that is worth noticing. It is often less a sign of the workload and more a sign of how long the mask has been on. It is closely tied to the habit of showing up as fine when you are not, only turned inward.

So what actually helps?

The unhelpful advice is to simply be more authentic, which usually just adds one more performance to manage. What actually shifts things is smaller and more honest. It starts with seeing how much of your day is surface acting rather than real work, because most people badly underestimate it, and you cannot ration something you cannot see. It continues with protecting a few relationships and moments where the face is allowed to come all the way off, since the harm comes from having no exit, not from ever wearing a face at all.

Where a real conversation comes in

Notice what the research is really pointing at. Not a flaw in you, and not a soft skill to buy. A working life that asks you to hold a face for hours with no place to set it down, and a quiet belief that being wrung out by it is simply the cost of being good. That belief is hard to question alone, because the same competence that built the mask is very good at defending it.

What tends to help is not another tip. It is a real, two-way conversation with someone whose only job in that hour is to listen and help you think, a space where you do not have to manage how you are coming across, so the vague feeling of being hollow can become a clear picture of what is actually draining you. That is a fair description of what good coaching and counselling are for. For many people it is the first hour in a long time where the face is allowed to come all the way off.

If you read this and recognised the wrung-out feeling that the day alone does not explain, that is worth taking seriously, and worth talking through with a person rather than performing your way past for another year.

Common questions about surface acting and emotional labour

What is surface acting at work?

Surface acting is showing an emotion you do not actually feel, such as smiling at a client while quietly furious, or projecting calm confidence in a meeting while anxious underneath. Researchers separate it from deep acting, where you genuinely try to feel the emotion you need to show. Study after study finds surface acting is the costly one, because you are managing the gap between your face and your feeling all day long.

Does faking emotions at work cause burnout?

The evidence points that way. A 2025 study of 1,506 workers in Frontiers in Organizational Psychology found that surface acting mediated the link between emotional demands and burnout, meaning the more people faked feelings they did not have, the more exhausted and disengaged they became, which in turn lowered their mental wellbeing. The pattern held across different professions and across every age group tested.

Is surface acting linked to depression?

A 2020 two-year prospective study in the International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health followed care workers over time and found that those high in surface acting at the start were at greater risk of developing depressive symptoms later. Because it measured the faking first and the low mood afterwards, it suggests the daily masking contributes to the mood, not just that low people mask more.

How is surface acting different from healthy professional composure?

Composure that costs you little tends to line up with something you actually feel, such as real care for a colleague or genuine interest in the work. Surface acting is the version where the face and the feeling have fully split, and you hold that split for hours with no place to set it down. The tiredness afterwards is the tell. If you feel hollowed out rather than simply busy, the emotional labour, not the workload, is often the reason.
