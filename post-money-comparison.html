You out-earn most people you know.
So why do you still feel behind?

On money & mental health · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

It usually arrives in an ordinary moment. A batchmate's name in a funding headline. An old colleague's new title on a screen you didn't mean to keep scrolling. A friend mentions, lightly, the size of the place they just moved into. Nothing is aimed at you. And still, something in your chest does a small, familiar drop, and the thought lands before you can stop it: everyone seems to be further ahead than me.

Which is strange, because on paper you are doing well. You earn more than most people you grew up with. More than you did five years ago. More, quietly, than you once thought would finally feel like enough. And none of that shows up in the moment. The number does not defend you. The feeling of being behind walks straight past it.

If you have quietly wondered what is wrong with you that a good income still leaves you feeling like you are losing a race, the honest answer is: nothing. New research suggests the feeling is not a flaw in your gratitude. It is doing exactly what human beings are built to do, and once you can see the mechanism, it stops being a private failing and starts being something you can actually work with.

The study that put a number on the feeling

In 2026, a team led by Frank Elgar at McGill University published a study in the journal Social Science & Medicine with a title that reads almost like a diagnosis: Left out and languishing. They analysed data from more than 200,000 people across 22 countries, drawn from one of the largest wellbeing surveys ever run, and they were asking a specific question. Not how much people earn, but whether people feel poorer than those around them, and what that feeling does to a life.

The finding is the part worth sitting with. People who felt worse off than their peers scored lower on nearly everything the researchers measured: happiness, health, a sense of meaning and purpose, the quality of their relationships, and even their sense of financial stability. And here is the detail that should reframe how you read your own bank app. This held true even when their actual income was similar to the peers they were comparing themselves against. The money was the same. The feeling was not.

It was never really about how much you have. It was about how much you have compared to the people you have quietly decided to measure yourself against.

What relative deprivation actually is

Researchers call this relative deprivation, and it is one of the more stubborn facts about human beings. We do not experience our circumstances against some fixed, absolute ruler. We experience them against a reference group: the people we treat, often without deciding to, as the fair comparison for someone like us. Your salary does not feel like a number. It feels like a rank. And a rank needs a table of other people to sit in.

This is why the raise never quite fixes it. When your income goes up, the people you compare yourself against tend to move up too, because success quietly changes the room you are standing in. You leave behind the friends who used to be the benchmark and start measuring against founders, partners, and the ones whose exits made the news. The reference group climbs with you, so the gap you are staring at never actually closes. You are running faster on a track that keeps tilting to stay just ahead of you.

Why it hits harder the higher you climb

There is a cruel logic to the fact that this often gets worse, not better, as you succeed. The more senior you become, the more your world fills with genuinely high achievers, because those are now your colleagues, your clients, your peers. The average person in your reference group is no longer the average person. It is a curated set of the most successful people you have ever met. Measured against that, almost anyone would feel a step behind, no matter how far they have actually come.

Add to this the fact that comparison today runs on incomplete information. You are matching your full interior, the doubts, the debt, the parts that did not work out, against everyone else's edited exterior. Their headline, not their private ledger. Their announcement, not their anxiety. It is not a fair fight, and some quiet part of you knows it, which is exactly why the comparison stings and refuses to resolve. You are losing to a version of other people's lives that does not fully exist.

The first honest question: are you actually behind, or does it just feel that way?

When the feeling of being behind gets loud, it is worth splitting it into two very different questions, because they get fused into one ache and treated as if they have a single answer. They do not.

The first is a real, factual question: given what you actually earn, spend, and hold, how safe are you? Most people carrying money anxiety have never actually calculated this, because the fear feels too big to look at directly, and so it stays vague, which lets it quote itself far higher than the truth. For a lot of people who feel like they are losing the race, the number that comes back is steadier than the panic had been suggesting, because uncertainty always prices itself higher than reality does.

The harder question: whose scoreboard are you even playing on?

The second question has no calculator, because it is not really about money. It is this: the race you feel like you are losing, did you ever actually choose to enter it? Or did you inherit the scoreboard, absorb it from your family, your batch, your industry, the specific corner of the internet you keep scrolling, and start keeping score by numbers you never sat down and agreed to?

This is the quietly liberating part of the research. If the injury is the comparison rather than the income, then the lever that actually moves things is not earning more. It is getting deliberate about who, and what, you are measuring against, and noticing when the answer is a reference group you would never have picked on purpose. That is genuinely hard to do alone, not because you lack the intelligence for it, but because the scoreboard feels like reality itself when you are standing inside it. It looks like the truth, not like a choice.

Seeing it mapped against your own answers is often the first time the scoreboard stops looking like a fact and starts looking like something you are allowed to put down.

What actually helps

None of this is an argument for lowering your standards or pretending you do not care about doing well. It is an argument for accuracy. The feeling of being behind is real, but it is information about your reference group, not a verdict on your life. Treat it that way and it loses most of its power over you.

In practice, the shift usually starts with two quiet questions asked honestly. What would enough actually look like, in numbers and in life, if no one else were watching? And who did you hand the job of grading you, and would you hire them for it today? Most people have never been asked either one out loud. Asked, and answered slowly with someone who is not inside your life, they tend to loosen a grip you had stopped noticing was there. Not budgeting advice, and not a pep talk about gratitude. Just a second, steadier vantage point on a race that, from the inside, is very hard to see clearly.

Common questions about feeling behind financially

Why do I feel behind financially even though I earn a good salary?

Because wellbeing tracks your position relative to the people you compare yourself with, not your salary in isolation. A 2026 study in Social Science & Medicine of more than 200,000 people across 22 countries found that people who felt poorer than their peers scored lower on happiness, health, meaning, relationships and even financial stability, and this held even when their actual income was similar to those peers. The feeling of being behind is real. It is just measuring against a reference group, not against your bank balance.

What is relative deprivation and how does it affect mental health?

Relative deprivation is the sense of having less than the people you measure yourself against, regardless of how much you actually have. The 2026 Elgar study named the pattern "left out and languishing": across 22 countries, feeling relatively deprived was linked to lower flourishing on multiple measures at once. It matters for mental health because comparison, not absolute income, is doing much of the work, which is why a raise can change your number without changing the feeling.

Does comparing my income to my peers actually harm my wellbeing?

The evidence points that way. In the 2026 cross-national study, the perception of being financially worse off than peers predicted lower wellbeing independent of how much a person earned. For senior professionals, the reference group is often the most successful people they know, batchmates who founded companies or made partner, which keeps the comparison permanently unflattering no matter how well things are actually going.
