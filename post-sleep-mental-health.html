You treat five hours of sleep as discipline.
Your mind has been treating it as damage.

On sleep & mental health · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

You have made your peace with it. The alarm goes at five something, the laptop closes near midnight, and the four or five hours in between are the tax you pay to stay ahead. You have said it out loud, maybe with a little pride. You do not need much sleep. You will sleep when the quarter is over. Sleep is for people with less on the line.

Here is the part that is worth saying plainly. The largest recent look at this question found that adults who sleep five hours or less are far more likely to be depressed than those who sleep six to eight, and the cost is not softness or laziness. It is a measurable hit to the exact clarity, steadiness and judgement your work depends on. The sleep you keep skipping is not free. Your mind has been quietly keeping the account.

Does not getting enough sleep really affect your mental health?

In January 2026, Dr Temitope Ogundare at Boston University School of Medicine published a study in the journal PLOS One using nationally representative data on 318,000 American adults, gathered between 2016 and 2023. He sorted people into three groups: short sleepers at five hours or less, a recommended group at six to eight hours, and long sleepers at nine hours or more. Then he compared their mental and physical health.

The short sleepers stood out. Compared with the six to eight hour group, sleeping five hours or less was associated with roughly a 14 percentage point higher rate of diagnosed depression, along with more days each month of poor mental health and poor physical health. Sleeping nine hours or more was linked to worse outcomes too. The healthiest place to be, across every measure, was the unglamorous middle. This is association, not proof that one causes the other on its own, but it is a very large, very consistent signal, and it points the same way every other sleep study does.

But isn't it just that anxious, low people sleep badly?

That is the obvious objection, and it is half right. Low mood does wreck sleep, anyone who has lain awake at 3am running the same worry on a loop knows that. But the arrow also runs the other way, and here the evidence is not a survey, it is an experiment.

In 2024, a team led by Dr Cara Palmer published a review in Psychological Bulletin that pooled 154 experimental studies covering more than 5,700 people whose sleep was deliberately cut short or broken up in a lab. When you take sleep away from people on purpose and watch what happens, the result is remarkably consistent. Every kind of sleep loss, a full night gone, a few hours trimmed, a night of interruptions, reduced positive emotions like joy and contentment and pushed up anxiety. Because the researchers controlled the sleep, this is cause and effect, not correlation. You do not have to be depressed for a bad night to flatten your mood and sharpen your worry. That is simply what a tired brain does.

Short sleep does not announce itself as a mental health problem. It arrives as a slightly shorter fuse, a joyless win, a small decision that takes twice the effort, and you blame the week instead of the sleep you traded away to survive it.

But you function fine on five hours, don't you?

This is the quietly dangerous part. One of the first things sleep loss damages is your ability to judge how impaired you are. You feel basically fine, so you keep signing off on the trade, while your reaction time, emotional control and decision quality slide underneath you. The senior professionals I speak with rarely arrive saying they have a sleep problem. They arrive irritable with people they love, strangely numb about work they used to care for, unable to enjoy the very success the missed sleep was meant to protect. When we trace it back, the nights are almost always part of the story.

Two related pieces on this site sit right next to this one. One is on why your mind stays at your desk long after you have left it, the rumination that eats the first hour of sleep. The other is on why you stay up scrolling even when you are exhausted, the stolen hour you take back at night because the day gave you nothing for yourself. Between them they explain most of how a capable adult ends up chronically short on the one thing that would help the most.

How common is this, really, among people like you?

Common enough that it has stopped looking like a problem. A 2025 survey by LocalCircles found a majority of the Indians it polled were getting less than six hours of uninterrupted sleep a night. When almost everyone around you is running on the same deficit, five hours stops feeling like a risk and starts feeling like the price of being serious. That is exactly why it is worth naming. A norm is not the same as a safe level, and the data does not grade on a curve. Your brain needs what it needs whether or not your peers are getting it either.

There is a harder version of this too. Short sleep does not only lower today's mood, it wears on the body over years, which is the through-line in a separate piece here on the physical toll chronic stress and poor recovery leave behind, and in one on what very long working weeks may do to the brain. Sleep is where a great deal of that repair is supposed to happen. Skip it long enough and the repair simply does not get done.

So what actually helps?

The honest answer is that most people do not have a sleep problem, they have a life that has quietly crowded sleep out, and those need different fixes. Sleep hygiene advice, a darker room, a consistent bedtime, less screen at night, is real and worth doing, but it rarely touches the deeper reason the hours vanished. If your nights are short because your mind will not stop working, or because the only time that feels like yours is after everyone else is asleep, the lever is not your bedroom, it is your load.

Where a real conversation comes in

Notice what the research is really pointing at. Not a willpower failure, and not a gadget to buy. A life arranged so that the first thing sacrificed is the thing that keeps your mind steady, and a story you tell yourself, that this is just what ambition costs, that keeps the arrangement in place. That story is hard to question alone, because the same drive that built the schedule is very good at defending it.

What tends to help is not another tip. It is a real, two-way conversation with someone whose only job in that hour is to listen and help you think, so that the vague sense of being tired and flat becomes a clear picture of what is actually draining you and what you want to change. That is a fair description of what good coaching and counselling are for.

If you read this while running on too little sleep and recognised yourself in it, that is worth taking seriously, and it is worth talking through with a person rather than pushing through for another year.

Common questions about sleep and mental health

Does not getting enough sleep affect your mental health?

Yes, and the link is strong. A 2026 study in PLOS One of 318,000 U.S. adults found that people sleeping five hours or less were about 14 percentage points more likely to have been diagnosed with depression than those sleeping the recommended six to eight hours, and reported more poor mental health days each month. Both too little and too much sleep were tied to worse mental health, so the healthiest range sat in the middle.

Does poor sleep cause low mood, or does low mood cause poor sleep?

It runs both ways, but the causal direction from sleep to mood is now well established. A 2024 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin pooled 154 experiments with 5,715 people who had their sleep deliberately shortened or broken. Across all of them, sleep loss reliably reduced positive emotions like joy and contentment and increased anxiety. Because the sleep was manipulated on purpose, the study shows losing sleep changes emotion, not just the other way around.

How much sleep do you actually need?

For most adults the healthiest range is about seven to nine hours, and in the 2026 PLOS One study the group sleeping six to eight hours had the best mental and physical health. Sleeping five hours or less, and sleeping nine or more, were both linked to higher depression and more unhealthy days. The goal is not maximum sleep, it is landing consistently in that middle band.

Why do busy professionals ignore how little they sleep?

Because short sleep is often worn as a sign of drive and discipline rather than seen as a health risk. Sleep loss also dulls your own insight into how impaired you are, so you feel fine while your judgement and mood quietly slip. In India a 2025 LocalCircles survey found a majority of respondents getting under six hours of uninterrupted sleep, which makes running on empty feel normal instead of costly.
