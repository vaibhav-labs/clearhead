AI was supposed to hand you your time back.
So why is the workday longer than ever?

On AI & workload · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

The pitch was simple, and you believed it, because it sounded like relief. Let the tools draft the email, summarise the deck, clean up the code, and you would finally get your evenings back. So you learned the tools. You got faster. On paper you are producing more than you ever have. And yet the day feels heavier, not lighter, and somewhere along the way you started to wonder whether the exhaustion is a you-problem.

Here is what the newest research says, plainly. For most people, AI is not giving time back. It is intensifying the work. A 2026 Harvard Business Review study found that AI tools did not shrink workloads. They sped up the pace, widened what each person took on, and stretched the day into more hours. You are not imagining the heaviness. The job got denser, and almost nobody warned you it would.

Is AI actually lightening your workload, or quietly adding to it?

In February 2026, Aruna Ranganathan, an associate professor at UC Berkeley's Haas School of Business, and her colleague Xingqi Maggie Ye published research in Harvard Business Review that followed employees at a technology company as they adopted AI at work. The promise going in was the familiar one, that the tools would remove routine work and free people up for the good stuff. What they actually found was the opposite. In their words, AI tools did not reduce work, they consistently intensified it. People worked at a faster pace, took on a broader scope of tasks, and extended work into more hours of the day, often without being asked to.

The detail that matters most is that adoption was voluntary. Nobody stood over these workers piling on more. The intensification came from the tool itself. One mechanism the researchers name is task expansion. Because AI can fill the gaps in what you know, you start reaching into work that was never yours, the analysis you would once have handed off, the design you would have left to someone else. The boundary of your job quietly widens until you are accountable for more than you ever agreed to carry. The result, in their framing, is workload creep, cognitive fatigue, and eventually burnout and weaker decisions. This lines up with Upwork's earlier finding that 77 percent of employees said AI had actually increased their workload rather than easing it.

Why do you feel more productive and more drained at the same time?

This is the part that makes people quietly doubt themselves. In 2025, the Upwork Research Institute surveyed 2,500 workers across the US, UK, Australia and Canada. On average, people using AI reported a 40 percent boost in productivity. That is real, and it is why the tools are not going anywhere. But the same study found something uncomfortable sitting right next to the gains. The workers reporting the highest AI-driven productivity were also the most burned out. Eighty-eight percent of them said they were experiencing burnout, and they were twice as likely to be thinking about quitting as their less AI-heavy peers.

Sit with that pairing for a second, because it explains a lot. The people getting the most out of AI are not coasting. They are the most exhausted and the closest to leaving. The output is visible, it shows up in what you ship and what your manager sees. The depletion is invisible, it shows up at 9pm when you cannot form a sentence and do not know why. So you do the natural thing and read the tiredness as a personal failing, a stamina problem, a sign you should push harder. It is not. It is the predictable cost of a job that got denser while the day stayed the same length.

AI did not hand your time back. It raised the bar on what a day is supposed to hold, then gave you faster tools to keep clearing it.

How does producing more turn into feeling worse?

The path from more output to worse mental health is not mysterious. A faster pace and a wider scope keep the mind switched on with fewer and fewer gaps to recover in. Every minute the tool saves gets refilled with another task, so the relief never actually lands. Over time that shows up as the cognitive fatigue and weaker decision-making the Berkeley researchers described, and eventually as low mood. This is the same current running under a related pattern worth reading here, the strain of constantly keeping up with AI, which a 2025 peer-reviewed study tied directly to measurable anxiety and depression symptoms. The technology is not the villain. The unbroken intensity is.

There is a quieter cost too. When the tool does the first draft of everything, you can end up feeling faster but less like yourself at work, which is the thread in a separate piece on whether leaning on AI is dulling your edge. Speed and depletion can rise together while the sense of doing work that is actually yours slowly thins out. That combination, more done and less felt, is a large part of why a productive year can still leave you flat.

You are senior, so shouldn't AI make your job easier?

This is the trap for experienced people, and it is a cruel one. You would expect seniority plus powerful tools to equal breathing room. Instead the intensification often lands hardest at the top. Task expansion scales with capability, so the more you can do, the more finds its way to you. On top of your actual role you are now expected to review more AI-assisted output, move faster because everyone assumes the tools made it quick, and visibly model adoption so your team follows. The freed-up time never arrives because the expectations rose to meet the new speed before you could feel any slack.

There is a disorientation that comes with it. In the same Upwork research, 62 percent of the highest-performing AI users said they did not understand how their daily use of AI connected to their organisation's actual goals. Read that again as a senior person. You are working faster than ever, carrying a wider scope than ever, and a majority of the strongest users cannot see what it is all adding up to. That is not a motivation problem. It is a system that rewarded output and forgot to say why. When the pace is high and the meaning is thin, the work gets reactive, and the reactive days are the ones that quietly hollow you out. If your role also asks you to deliver far more than you truly control, the piece on why you cannot switch off from work sits right alongside this one.

What actually helps when you cannot just switch AI off?

The honest answer is not to use AI less, because for most roles that ship has sailed and pretending otherwise just adds guilt. The move is to stop treating AI as a flood to survive and start treating it as a scope to manage. Three levers do real work here. The first is to redraw the boundary of your job on purpose. Task expansion happened by accident, so decide deliberately what is genuinely yours to own and what quietly crept in, and hand back or renegotiate the rest instead of silently absorbing it.

The second lever is recovery. The intensity does its damage by removing the gaps, so protect them like they are the work, because in a real sense they are. Do not let every minute the tool saves get refilled on reflex. The third lever is confidence, and it is more powerful than it sounds. A 2024 study in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications found that AI adoption raised job stress and burnout, but that employees who felt genuinely capable of learning the tools were far less affected. Fluency you build on purpose, rather than a flood you brace against, changes how the same technology lands on your nervous system.

Where a real conversation comes in

Notice what the research is really pointing at. Not a discipline failure, and not a course to buy. A working life that got denser almost without anyone deciding it should, a scope that widened one convenient task at a time, and an old story running underneath it that says any strain just means you should be more efficient. That story is what keeps you doing the one thing that cannot fix an intensity problem, which is going faster.

What tends to help is not another productivity hack. It is a real, two-way conversation with someone whose only job in that hour is to listen and help you think, so that the vague sense of drowning becomes a clear map of what you actually chose to carry, what the tools quietly added, and what you can set down without letting anyone down. That is a fair description of what good coaching and counselling are for.

If you read this while producing more than ever and feeling worse than you can explain, that is worth taking seriously, and it is worth talking through with a person rather than pushing through for another quarter.

Common questions about AI, workload and burnout

Is AI actually reducing my workload?

For most people, not really. A 2026 Harvard Business Review study by researchers at UC Berkeley found that AI tools did not shrink workloads. Instead people worked at a faster pace, took on a wider scope of tasks, and stretched work into more hours of the day, often without being asked. The tools removed some drudgery, then the freed-up time quietly filled with more work. This is why you can produce more and still feel busier than before.

Why am I more productive with AI but also more burned out?

Because output and depletion are rising together. In a 2025 Upwork Research Institute survey of 2,500 workers, people using AI reported a 40 percent productivity boost, yet the workers with the biggest gains were the most burned out, with 88 percent reporting burnout and twice the intention to quit. The productivity is visible on your screen. The depletion is invisible, so it is easy to read it as a personal failing rather than a predictable cost.

Does using AI at work affect mental health?

It can, through the strain it adds rather than the technology itself. Peer-reviewed 2024 and 2025 studies have linked AI-related job stress and technostress to higher burnout, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. The pattern is consistent. A faster pace and a wider scope of responsibility keep the mind switched on, recovery gaps disappear, and the wear shows up as low mood and exhaustion. Confidence in your own ability to learn the tools appears to soften the effect.

What can I do if I cannot just stop using AI?

Stop treating it as a flood to survive and start treating it as a scope to manage. Decide what is genuinely yours to own and what task expansion quietly added, protect real recovery gaps instead of refilling every saved minute, and build AI fluency deliberately so it feels like a skill rather than a threat. A structured conversation with a coach or counsellor helps you separate the load you chose from the load that crept in, before it hardens into burnout.
