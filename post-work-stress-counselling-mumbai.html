Counselling for work stress in Mumbai:
what actually helps

On pressure & burnout · 9 min read · Clearhead, Andheri West, Mumbai · Published 10 Oct 2026

If you typed "counsellor for work stress Mumbai" into a search bar, something has been building for a while. The job hasn't broken you. It has stopped fitting, and you have nobody to think it through with who isn't also inside it.

Counselling for work stress in Mumbai is a series of private, one-to-one conversations, in person in Andheri West or online, in which you work out what the job is doing to you and what you can change. It is not medical care. If sleep, mood or anxiety have been off for weeks, see a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist first.

This page covers what work stress looks like in this city, when a clinician should see it, what the sessions involve, who does what, how to choose someone, and when Clearhead is not the right place.

What does work stress look like in Mumbai's corporate life?

Mostly it looks like a normal week. The 8:40 train or the Western Express Highway, calls that run past seven, a group chat that lights up through dinner, and a mind still at the desk at eleven. None of it is dramatic on its own. The weight comes from the fact that it never stops.

The hours are long by any international measure. The ILO's 2023 report on working time found that Southern Asia, which includes India, had the longest average working week of any region in the world at 49.0 hours in 2019, against a global average of 43.9. The ILO treats anything over 48 hours a week as long hours. The average week here is over that line before anyone opens a laptop at home.

Then there is the commute. In TomTom's 2025 traffic data for Mumbai, a 10 km drive took 35 and a half minutes in the evening rush hour, and a typical driver lost 126 hours to rush-hour traffic across the year: more than five full days spent neither at work nor at home.

And there is what it adds up to. In Deloitte's 2022 survey of 3,995 employees across corporate India, more than 80% reported at least one symptom of poor mental health in the previous year. Among those who did, the workplace was the single biggest source of stress, named by 47%, ahead of money, the pandemic and family. More than 55% said they felt emotionally exhausted or burnt out, and over half reported trouble falling or staying asleep. Of those who did something about it, only 34% went to a professional.

Mumbai is not uniquely bad. But it makes one shape of stress easy to fall into: too many hours, too little recovery, and an unspoken rule that you don't say so out loud.

Is it stress, or something a clinician should see?

This is the question to settle before you choose anyone, and the one I ask first.

Work stress and burnout are responses to a situation. The WHO's ICD-11 definition of burnout describes a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with three features: exhaustion, growing mental distance or cynicism about the job, and a sense of being less effective. The WHO is explicit that it is an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. That is the territory counselling is for.

Depression, an anxiety disorder or problem drinking are different. They can start with work stress and still be there after the situation changes. India's National Mental Health Survey of 34,802 adults put the current prevalence of any mental morbidity at 10.56%, found it higher in urban metros like Mumbai, and found that 84.5% of people with a condition were getting no treatment at all. Most of them were not in crisis. They were getting through.

A counsellor cannot diagnose, and I will not pretend to. These are the signs that a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist should see you first, or alongside:

  • Low mood or anxiety that has lasted most days for several weeks, including days off
  • Sleep, appetite or concentration that have changed in a way that is not recovering
  • Using alcohol or anything else to get through the evening or the morning
  • Panic that arrives without a trigger, or a sense of dread about ordinary things
  • Any thought of harming yourself. In that case, call Tele-MANAS on 14416, the Government of India's free, 24/7 mental health line, or go to the nearest emergency department

If none of that is true, and what you have is a job that has quietly become too heavy, you are in the right place to keep reading.

What does counselling for work stress actually involve, session by session?

People picture either a couch or a whiteboard. It is neither. Here is what a short block of work typically looks like at Clearhead. The order is roughly right; the content is always yours.

  1. The free first conversation (30 minutes). You say what is going on, in whatever shape it comes out. I listen, ask a few questions, and tell you honestly whether this is the right kind of help. No payment, no pitch at the end.
  2. Session one: naming it. We slow the week down and look at where the pressure actually comes from. Hours, a particular manager, a role that has outgrown its title, or carrying something nobody sees. The answer is usually more specific than people expect.
  3. Session two: what is yours to change. Some of the stress is structural and will not move. Some is a boundary you have not drawn, a conversation you have not had, or a standard nobody asked you to hold. We sort one from the other.
  4. Sessions three and four: trying something. One or two concrete changes, tested between sessions, then reviewed. A different end to the working day. A message left until morning. A conversation that had been put off.
  5. If you continue: the longer questions. Sometimes the stress is a symptom of a bigger question about direction or identity. That is where the six- and twelve-session blocks come in, and where the work moves from counselling towards coaching.

Sessions are spaced two to three weeks apart, with WhatsApp support between them if you want it, because the change happens in the weeks, not in the room.

What happens in a first conversation?

Less than you fear. You do not need a tidy summary. "I don't know if this is a work problem or a me problem" is plenty.

I will ask how long it has been like this, what a bad week looks like, what you have already tried, and whether anything in the clinical list above applies. By the end of the half hour you should know whether this is stress or something that needs a clinician, whether talking to me is likely to help, and what the next step would be. If someone else would serve you better, you will hear that too.

How do you choose someone in Mumbai?

There is no licensing body in India for the words "coach" or "counsellor", so the checking falls to you. A short list:

  1. Ask what they are trained in, and who certifies it. For coaching, look for an International Coaching Federation credential. By ICF's own comparison, a PCC has completed at least 125 hours of coach education, 500 hours of coaching experience and 10 hours of mentor coaching, and must renew every three years; ICF keeps a public "Verify a Coach" directory. For a clinical psychologist, ask for their Central Rehabilitation Register number. Under Section 13 of the Rehabilitation Council of India Act, nobody may practise as a clinical psychologist in India without being on that register, which RCI publishes online.
  2. Ask what they will not do. A good answer sounds like "I don't diagnose, and here is when I'd refer you." A bad answer promises outcomes.
  3. Ask about confidentiality in plain terms. Who else sees your notes? What happens if your employer is paying?
  4. Ask for the full fee before you commit. Fees in Mumbai vary a great deal with training, experience and format, and I have not found a reliable public benchmark, so I will not invent one. Clearhead's fees are on the pricing page: between ₹5,500 and ₹6,500 per 60-minute session depending on the package. If you are weighing the money, this piece on the cost of getting help, and of waiting, is the honest version.
  5. Use the first conversation as the real test. Credentials get you in the door. Whether you can say the true thing to this person in the first twenty minutes decides it.

Online or in person: which is better for work stress?

Neither, as a rule. Online removes the commute, which in Mumbai is not a small thing, and some people are more honest from their own room. In person suits people who have spent all day on screens and want one conversation that is not a call.

At Clearhead both are equal options, and you can switch from session to session. In-person sessions are at Lokhandwala Complex, Andheri West, Monday to Saturday, 9:00 to 19:00 IST; the Andheri West page has the practical details. Online sessions reach clients across the rest of Mumbai, India and abroad.

When Clearhead is not the right fit

I would rather say this here than in the first conversation. Clearhead is not the right place if:

  • You are in crisis, or have had thoughts of harming yourself. Call 14416 or go to an emergency department. That comes before anything on this page.
  • You have, or suspect you have, a clinical condition and are not yet under a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist. Get that assessment first. Counselling can sit alongside clinical care, not replace it.
  • You want someone to tell you what to do. You will leave with your own answer rather than mine.
  • You need a quick fix before a deadline. Sessions are spaced two to three weeks apart on purpose.
  • You want your employer to receive a report. What you say stays between us.

If none of those apply, and the job has simply become too heavy to carry alone, this is what the practice is for.

Work stress rarely needs fixing. It needs saying out loud, to someone who will not flinch and will not report it upstairs.

What to take from this

Check the clinical list first, honestly. If anything on it applies, see a clinician this week. If nothing does, the next step is a conversation, not a commitment: thirty minutes, free, in Andheri West or online.

Common questions about work stress counselling in Mumbai

Do you offer counselling for work stress in person in Mumbai?

Yes. In-person sessions happen at Lokhandwala Complex, Andheri West, Mumbai 400053, Monday to Saturday, 9:00 to 19:00 IST. Online sessions are offered as an equal option for people elsewhere in Mumbai, across India or abroad, and you can switch between the two from one session to the next.

Is work stress counselling the same as therapy?

No. Counselling for work stress is a structured, confidential conversation about what the job is doing to you and what you can change. It does not diagnose or treat a mental health condition. If low mood, anxiety or disrupted sleep have lasted weeks and are affecting daily life, a psychiatrist or an RCI-registered clinical psychologist is the right first stop, and counselling can sit alongside that care rather than replace it.

How many sessions does counselling for work stress usually take?

It varies, and nobody can promise a number. At Clearhead the work is offered in blocks of 4, 6 or 12 sessions of 60 minutes, spaced two to three weeks apart. Many people find four sessions enough to name the problem and change one or two things; questions about direction or identity tend to need longer.

What does counselling for work stress cost in Mumbai?

Fees in Mumbai vary widely with the practitioner's training, experience and format, and there is no reliable public benchmark, so ask for the full fee before you commit. Clearhead's fees are listed openly on the pricing page: between ₹5,500 and ₹6,500 per 60-minute session depending on the package, and the first 30-minute conversation is free.

When should I see a psychiatrist instead of a counsellor for work stress?

See a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist first if the low mood or anxiety has lasted weeks, if sleep, appetite or concentration have changed in a lasting way, if you are using alcohol to get through, or if you have had thoughts of harming yourself. In a crisis, call Tele-MANAS on 14416, the Government of India's free, 24/7 mental health line, or go to the nearest emergency department.
