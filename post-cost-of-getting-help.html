Getting help costs money.
So does waiting.

On money & the cost of waiting · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai · Published 4 Oct 2026

You have the pricing page open in one tab and your banking app in the other. You do the sum twice. It's less than the weekend away you booked without a second thought. Still, a voice says wait. It isn't that bad yet.

Is getting help worth the money? Usually, yes, and when you start matters almost as much as what you spend. A WHO-led study estimated that every US$1 invested in treating depression and anxiety returns US$4 in better health and ability to work, and studies of treatment delay find that depression treated sooner tends to respond better.

That doesn't make the worry silly. Money is a real constraint, and some help is poor value. But most people price the fees down to the rupee and never write down what the waiting costs.

Why does paying for help feel harder than paying for anything else?

Many senior professionals pay for a physiotherapist or a CA without much agonising. Both keep life running, and nobody thinks less of you for needing one. Paying someone to help with what's going on in your head lands differently. It can feel like an indulgence, or like an admission.

Deloitte's 2022 survey of 3,995 employees in corporate India shows how this plays out. More than 80% reported at least one symptom of poor mental health. Of those who did something about it, only 34% went to a professional, while half used self-help techniques such as yoga or meditation and 48% leaned on friends and family. Among people who took no action at all, about a quarter were afraid of what it would do to their image, and roughly one in five said help was out of reach. One young employee told the researchers, simply, "Counsellors are very expensive."

So the money worry is real. It rarely travels alone, though. A 2025 review of why people put off care for depression found that attitudinal barriers, such as stigma and not recognising symptoms for what they are, were often a stronger brake than cost or access. Price is often just the easiest reason to say out loud.

What does waiting actually cost?

Waiting is the default, and the wait is usually measured in years. The WHO World Mental Health Surveys interviewed 76,012 adults across 15 countries. Among people who eventually sought treatment, the median delay between a problem starting and the first treatment contact ranged from 1 to 14 years for mood disorders and from 3 to 30 years for anxiety disorders, depending on the country and the condition. A separate US survey put the delay at 6 to 8 years for mood disorders and 9 to 23 years for anxiety disorders. Closer to home, India's National Mental Health Survey found a treatment gap of 84.5%: most people with a mental health condition were getting no treatment at all.

No invoice arrives for any of this, but the cost is real. In the Deloitte study, a third of people kept working through poor mental health, and trouble concentrating and a slower pace of work were among the most common effects. Deloitte estimated the cost to Indian employers at about ₹1.1 lakh crore over the year it studied, a period swollen by the pandemic. The largest share, around ₹51,000 crore, came from presenteeism: people at their desks, working below their usual level. Worldwide, the WHO estimates that depression and anxiety cost 12 billion working days a year, or about US$1 trillion in lost productivity.

Those are employer figures. You pay your share in a currency spreadsheets miss, like the evenings spent turning the same problem over, or the decision that has been on hold for months. If money is part of what's weighing on you, that worry has its own cost to your attention, which I've written about in how money worry eats into focus.

Is there real evidence that getting help pays for itself?

At the level of whole populations, yes. A WHO-led analysis in The Lancet Psychiatry modelled scaling up treatment for depression and anxiety in 36 countries between 2016 and 2030. WHO summed up the result as a fourfold return: US$4 in better health and ability to work for every US$1 spent. Deloitte's 2024 UK report, which averaged 26 published studies of workplace wellbeing programmes, found employers got back about £4.70 for every £1.

For coaching, the strongest evidence comes from randomised trials. A 2023 meta-analysis in Academy of Management Learning & Education pooled 37 of them, covering 2,528 people, and found a moderate effect of workplace and executive coaching (g = 0.59) across personal and leadership outcomes. The authors also found signs of publication bias, which usually means the real effect is somewhat smaller than the pooled figure.

You will also see coaching sold on much bigger numbers. The International Coach Federation's 2009 client study reported a median return of 3.44 times the fee for individuals and 7 times for companies. I hold an ICF credential, and I still wouldn't lean on those figures. Only 9% of the 2,165 people surveyed could supply the numbers needed to work out a return, and the report itself says the results should be read with caution.

Why does starting early change the return?

A problem caught early is smaller. It hasn't yet spread into your sleep or your closest relationships, and you haven't spent a year building habits around avoiding it. Deloitte's researchers saw the late pattern clearly: employees tended to acknowledge their symptoms only once the effects showed up in their relationships, their productivity or their physical health.

The clinical research points the same way. A 2014 meta-analysis in the Journal of Affective Disorders found that people whose depression was treated sooner were about 1.7 times as likely to respond to treatment and 1.65 times as likely to reach remission, although it rested on a small pool of comparable studies. The 2025 review of treatment delay in depression linked longer waits with more severe symptoms and a weaker response to treatment.

Deloitte's UK analysis found something similar in employer returns. The highest returns came from early measures, such as culture change and education, rather than the more intensive support needed later, once someone is already struggling. One more detail from the coaching meta-analysis matters for your budget: the effect didn't depend much on how long the coaching ran. Longer wasn't reliably better.

The cheapest time to get help is usually a little earlier than it feels necessary.

How do you work out whether it's worth it for you?

Put both sides on the page. Most people fill in the first column down to the rupee and leave the second one blank.

If money is the tight constraint, put a number on that as well. If help still looks worth trying, treat it as a bounded experiment, with a set number of sessions and one clear question you want answered by the end. Some practitioners offer a free first conversation so you can judge the fit before you pay. At Clearhead that conversation is 30 minutes, so we both know whether this is the right fit before any money changes hands, and the packages are listed openly. Check what your employer already pays for, too. If there's an employee assistance programme, a few sessions of counselling may already be covered.

When is paid help the wrong first step?

Sometimes it is. If you're in crisis or having thoughts of harming yourself, coaching is the wrong place to start. Call Tele-MANAS on 14416, the Government of India's free, round-the-clock mental health line, or go to the nearest emergency department. If low mood or anxiety has lasted weeks and is disrupting your sleep or your work, see a doctor or a psychiatrist first. Coaching and counselling at Clearhead are not a substitute for clinical care.

And if money is tight this month, start with what's free, like Tele-MANAS or a programme your employer already pays for. Paying for help shouldn't become a fresh source of the stress you're trying to ease.

What to take from this

If a question has been sitting with you for months, count the months as well as the fees. Then take the smallest step that costs nothing, whether that's a free conversation or a few minutes with the runway calculator. You can decide about the money once you know what you're dealing with.

Common questions about paying for help

Is coaching or counselling worth the money?

Often, yes. A WHO-led study in The Lancet Psychiatry estimated a return of US$4 in better health and ability to work for every US$1 invested in treating depression and anxiety, and a 2023 meta-analysis of 37 randomised trials found a moderate effect of workplace coaching. Fit matters as much as method, so use a free first conversation to judge it before you pay.

Is it better to get help early or wait and see if it passes?

Some hard patches do pass on their own. But waiting tends to run long: in the WHO World Mental Health Surveys, the median delay before first treatment ranged from 1 to 14 years for mood disorders across countries. For depression, a 2014 meta-analysis found people treated sooner were more likely to respond and to reach remission. If something has lasted weeks and is affecting your sleep or your work, it is worth talking to someone.

What if I can't afford help right now?

Start with what is free. Tele-MANAS (14416) is the Government of India's free, round-the-clock mental health line. Check whether your employer covers counselling through an employee assistance programme. Some practitioners, Clearhead included, offer a free first conversation, and the free Abundance Runway Calculator can show you how many months your finances could carry you before you decide anything.

How do I know whether I need a coach, a counsellor or a doctor?

A coach helps with direction, decisions and how you are handling work and life. A counsellor helps with emotional difficulty that is weighing on you. If low mood or anxiety has lasted weeks and is disrupting your sleep or daily life, see a doctor or a psychiatrist first. If you are in crisis or having thoughts of harming yourself, call Tele-MANAS on 14416 or go to the nearest emergency department.
