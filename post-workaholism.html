It's not the hours you work.
It's that you can't put it down.

On workaholism, work addiction & the self-worth underneath it · 9 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

You told yourself you would stop at eight. It is now half past ten and you are still answering the message that could have waited until morning, still refreshing the thread, still clearing one more thing so that tomorrow feels survivable. You are tired in a way sleep does not fix. And underneath the tiredness there is a quieter feeling you rarely say out loud: that if you actually stopped, if you sat still with nothing to produce, you would not know who you were.

Here is the plain version. Workaholism is not working hard. It is a compulsion to work that you cannot switch off, even when it costs your health, your sleep, and the people you love. The most recent research suggests it rarely fades on its own, because what drives it is not the job in front of you. It is what you have quietly come to believe about yourself, and that is a far harder thing to close the laptop on.

What is workaholism, and how is it different from just working hard?

The difference is not the number of hours. It is whether you can put the work down. Working hard is a choice you make and can un-make. Workaholism, in the definition researchers now use, is an uncontrollable need to work and a loss of control over how much you do, that carries on even when it plainly harms you. The tell is not on your calendar. It is in what happens inside you when you try to stop: the restlessness, the guilt, the low static of anxiety that only quiets when you open the laptop again.

And it is not rare. The first systematic review and meta-analysis of workaholism, published in Frontiers in Psychology in 2023, pooled 71,625 people across 23 countries and put the prevalence at roughly 14 percent, about one in seven working adults. Read that again. In most teams you have ever sat in, the pattern was in the room. We just call it commitment and hand it a promotion.

Why can't I stop, even when I want to?

This is the question that matters, and 2025 gave us an unusually clear answer. Bernadette Kun and Gyongyi Kokonyei followed 586 workers over four years, measuring work addiction at two points in time, and published their findings in BMC Psychology. The value of a study that long is that it can separate people whose work addiction faded from those in whom it stayed chronic, and then ask what the chronic group had in common.

What they had in common was not longer hours or bigger jobs. It was a personality signature: higher perfectionism, lower self-esteem, more rumination, and more external, pressure-based motivation. In plain terms, the people who could not stop were the people whose sense of their own worth was tethered to their output. The work was not the addiction. The work was the proof. As long as slowing down feels like watching your own value drain away, no amount of willpower will make stopping feel safe.

A workaholic is not someone who loves their work too much. It is someone who has quietly decided they are not allowed to be a person when they are not producing.

What is it actually costing you?

More than a few tired mornings. In 2025, Catherine So-Kum Tang studied 1,233 full-time employees aged 25 to 65 and published the results in Frontiers in Psychology. The study traced a specific chain from work addiction to poor physical health. Work addiction wore down people's ability to regulate their own emotions. That emotional depletion pushed them toward addictive eating as a way to cope. And that, in turn, damaged their physical health, a loop that fed itself.

That is one measured pathway, and it sits on top of everything else the wider evidence already links to work addiction: more anxiety, more depressive symptoms, more burnout, more conflict at home, and less satisfaction with the very job you are sacrificing everything for. The cruel irony is built in. The behaviour you adopted to feel secure and in control is, over time, one of the more reliable ways to erode your health and your closest relationships. The body keeps the account even when you refuse to open it.

Why does this hit senior and high-achieving people hardest?

Because everything around you agrees with the addiction. Nobody stages an intervention for the person who is always available, always delivering, always the last one online. They give them more. The workplace does not just tolerate the pattern that is quietly hollowing you out. It celebrates it, promotes it, and holds it up as an example, which makes it almost impossible to see as a problem rather than an identity.

And the higher you rise, the more your name and the work fuse into one thing. The role becomes the answer to who you are, so stepping back from the role feels like stepping out of your own life. This is the same knot that runs under a lot of quiet high-performer distress. It sits next to the perfectionism that never lets anything be good enough, and to the way relentless overwork slowly changes how your brain works. Left running, it is a direct road to the kind of burnout that rest alone no longer touches. The problem was never that you lacked discipline. The problem is that discipline is exactly what kept the engine running.

Can this change, or is it just who I am?

It can change, and the same four-year study is the reason to believe it. Kun and Kokonyei did not find one fixed group of doomed workaholics. They found movement: people whose work addiction eased over time, alongside those in whom it stayed. What separated the two was not luck. It was the personal drivers underneath, the perfectionism, the fragile self-worth, the pressure-based motivation. Those are not life sentences. They are beliefs and patterns, and beliefs and patterns are workable.

The change is not a productivity hack, and it is not simply working less, which for a workaholic often becomes one more performance to get right. The real work is loosening the knot that ties your worth to your output, so that stopping stops feeling like disappearing. That starts with seeing the pattern clearly. Where a real conversation comes in

Notice what the research is really saying. The problem is not that you are lazy, and the answer is not more force. The problem is a quiet belief, learned long ago and reinforced daily, that you are only acceptable when you are producing. That belief is almost impossible to argue yourself out of alone, because the part of you doing the arguing is the same part that is terrified of stopping.

What tends to help is a real, two-way conversation with someone whose only job in that hour is to listen and help you think, a space where you are not being measured by anything, so the compulsion can finally be looked at instead of obeyed. That is a fair description of what good coaching and counselling are for. For a lot of driven people it is the first place in years where they are allowed to simply be a person, with nothing to deliver, and discover that the ground does not give way when they do.

If you read this and recognised the half past ten that was supposed to be eight, the tiredness that sleep does not reach, the fear of who you would be if you stopped, that is worth taking seriously, and worth talking through with a person rather than working through for one more night.

Common questions about workaholism and work addiction

What is workaholism, and how is it different from working hard?

Working hard is a choice you can put down. Workaholism is a compulsion you cannot. Researchers define it as an uncontrollable need to work, with a loss of control over how much you work, that continues even when it clearly harms your health, mood, and relationships. Someone who works hard can rest and feel fine. A workaholic tends to feel anxious, guilty, or empty the moment they stop, which is why the driver sits inside the person, not in the workload.

How common is work addiction?

More common than most people assume. The first systematic review and meta-analysis of workaholism prevalence, published in Frontiers in Psychology in 2023 and pooling 71,625 people across 23 countries, put the estimate at about 14 percent, or roughly one in seven working adults. It is not a rare disorder of a few extreme people. It is a widespread pattern that the modern workplace quietly rewards.

Why can't I stop working even when I want to?

Because for most people the compulsion is not really about the work. A 2025 four-year study of 586 workers in BMC Psychology found that people whose work addiction stayed chronic over time were marked by higher perfectionism, lower self-esteem, more rumination, and more external, pressure-based motivation. In other words, the engine is a belief that your worth has to be earned through output. As long as stopping feels like becoming worthless, stopping stays almost impossible.

What is workaholism doing to my health?

More than fatigue. A 2025 study of 1,233 full-time employees in Frontiers in Psychology found that work addiction wore down physical health along a chain: it depleted people's ability to regulate their emotions, which pushed them toward addictive eating as a way to cope, which in turn damaged their physical health. Work addiction also travels with higher anxiety, depression, burnout, and strained relationships. The body keeps the account even when you refuse to look at it.
