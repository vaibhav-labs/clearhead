You kept your job.
So why does it feel this heavy?

On layoffs & the ones who stay · 7 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

The email went out on a Tuesday. By Thursday the desks around you were emptier, a few chats had gone quiet, and someone you used to get coffee with was walking out with a laptop bag and a strange, careful smile. You kept your job. On paper, you are one of the lucky ones. So it makes very little sense that the days since have felt worse, not better, and that you have not been able to say so to anyone.

If that is roughly where you are, the first thing worth knowing is that this reaction is not a character flaw or a failure of perspective. It is a documented pattern, common enough that researchers gave it a name decades ago, and the shape of it explains almost everything you have been feeling.

Here is the short version. A layoff does not end when the announcement does. The people who keep their jobs are often left carrying guilt, anxiety, anger, and a quiet loss of trust, while also absorbing the work the departed used to do. Relief and grief show up at the same moment, and together they weigh more than either one would on its own.

What is layoff survivor syndrome?

The organizational psychologist Joel Brockner spent years studying the people left behind after downsizing, and named what he found survivor syndrome. His work, going back to a 1988 paper in Research in Organizational Behavior, described a familiar cocktail in the people who remained: guilt, anxiety, anger, distrust of the organization, and a drop in commitment and performance, even among those the company most wanted to keep. The finding that surprised managers then still surprises them now. Survival does not produce gratitude. It produces strain.

More recent numbers say the same thing in a plainer voice. A LeadershipIQ study of layoff survivors found that three of the words people reached for most to describe how they felt were anger, anxiety, and guilt, and that roughly three in four said their own productivity fell after the cuts. So if you are producing less while telling yourself you should be relieved and getting on with it, you are not the exception. You are the rule that has been measured.

A layoff removes people from the building. It does not remove them from the mind of everyone who stayed.

You still have your job. Why has the work gotten heavier?

Part of what you are feeling is not emotional at all. It is arithmetic. The work that three people used to do does not leave with them. It gets quietly redistributed to whoever is still at their desk, which now includes you, on top of what you already carried. Nobody sends a memo announcing this. It simply becomes the new normal, and you are expected to meet it without visibly straining, because straining might look like you are not one of the ones who deserved to stay.

The APA's 2025 Work in America survey, conducted by The Harris Poll among more than 2,000 US workers, captured the downstream effect. In organizations that had been through significant disruption, workers reported markedly higher rates of emotional exhaustion and of low motivation and energy than those in untouched workplaces. The load is real, the fear of complaining about it is real, and the two feed each other.

This is worth separating out from the guilt, because it responds to a different kind of help. Guilt needs to be spoken. Overload needs to be seen clearly and named as a capacity problem, not a personal failing. Is it normal to feel guilty when you kept your job?

It is so normal that the strangeness would be not feeling it. Survivor guilt is the private sense that you were spared for no reason you can fully defend, while people you respected, who worked as hard as you did, were not. Your mind goes looking for the logic of it, cannot find any that holds, and settles instead on a low, persistent unease. Why them and not me is a question with no answer that will let you rest, so it just keeps running.

The cruel part is how isolating it is. Because you kept your job, the socially approved response is relief, so the guilt goes unspoken. You cannot grieve openly, because you did not lose anything anyone can see. You cannot complain, because you still have what others just lost. So you carry it alone, which is the exact condition under which a feeling grows heavier rather than lighter.

Why the fear doesn't switch off once the cuts are done

You might have expected that once the round was over, the dread would lift. For most people it does not, and there is a reason. Before the layoff, job security was a background assumption you never had to think about. The layoff turned that assumption into a live question, and once a thing has happened once, the mind treats it as something that can happen again.

The data tracks this too. In the APA's 2025 survey, 54% of workers said job insecurity has a significant impact on their stress, and 44% said they feared an economic downturn could cost them their job within the year, up from 36% just twelve months earlier. That is the sound of a whole workforce bracing. If you want the fuller picture of how that particular fear works on the body and mind before anything has even happened, it is the subject of a separate piece here on the quiet cost of job insecurity.

And if you were the one who had to decide, or deliver, the cuts, there is a second weight most people never mention, because managers are assumed to be fine. You are not exempt from any of this. That specific, lonely strain is worth its own honest look, which is what happens to the people who hold the team together.

What actually helps

The first real relief is simply naming it, out loud, to someone who will not rush to reassure you. Almost everyone in this situation is running the same silent script, that they have no right to feel bad, so saying the true sentence, this is harder than I expected and I feel guilty even saying that, tends to loosen something on its own.

From there, it helps to pull apart three things that usually arrive fused into one heavy mood. There is grief, for people who left and for a team that will not be the same. There is fear, about your own footing and what comes next. And there is plain overload, the extra work that landed on you without ceremony. Each one is real, and each one asks for a different response. Grief wants to be felt and spoken. Fear wants to be looked at squarely rather than managed by not-looking. Overload wants to be measured and, where possible, renegotiated.

None of this means anything has gone wrong with you. It means you are having an ordinary, well documented response to a genuinely hard thing, and that the response is treatable with attention rather than willpower. This is not a substitute for clinical care, and if the weight is affecting your sleep, your health, or your ability to function, a doctor or a therapist is the right first call. But for the specific tangle of guilt, fear, and quiet overload that survivors carry, a steady conversation with someone outside the situation is often exactly the thing that helps you set it down.

Common questions about layoff survivor guilt

Why does surviving a layoff feel so bad?

Because a layoff does not end when the meeting does. Researchers call the aftermath survivor syndrome: the people who keep their jobs are often left with guilt, anxiety, anger, and a quiet loss of trust, while also absorbing the work the departed used to do. Relief and grief arrive at the same time, and the mix is heavier than either would be alone.

Is it normal to feel guilty after keeping your job while others were let go?

Yes, and it is common enough to have a name. In one study of layoff survivors, three of the words people used most for how they felt were anger, anxiety, and guilt. Survivor guilt is the private sense that you were spared for no reason you can defend, while people you respected were not. It is not weakness or ingratitude. It is a normal human response to an unfair-feeling outcome you did not control.

Why hasn't my stress gone away now that the layoffs are over?

Because the layoff proved the ground can move, so your mind keeps bracing for the next round. The APA's 2025 Work in America survey found 54% of workers say job insecurity significantly affects their stress, and 44% feared a downturn could cost them their job within a year, up from 36% the year before. Once you have watched it happen, security stops feeling like a fact and starts feeling like a guess.

What actually helps with layoff survivor guilt?

Naming it honestly is the first real relief, because most survivors carry it silently, assuming they have no right to struggle. From there it helps to separate three tangled things: the grief for people and a team that changed, the fear about your own future, and the simple overload of doing more with fewer hands. Talking it through with someone outside the company, who you do not have to reassure, is often where the knot loosens.
