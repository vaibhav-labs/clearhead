Coach, counsellor or therapist?
How to choose the right help in India.

On clarity & being heard · 10 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai · Published 10 Oct 2026

You've decided to talk to someone. Then the search results arrive: life coach, executive coach, counsellor, psychologist, therapist, psychiatrist. Half the websites use the words interchangeably. The other half seem to want you to pick a side before you've said a word.

The short version: a coach works on direction and how you handle work and life. A counsellor works on what is weighing on you emotionally. A clinical psychologist assesses and treats diagnosed conditions. A psychiatrist is a medical doctor, and the only one of the four who can prescribe. If your sleep, safety or daily functioning is affected, see a clinician first.

The rest of this piece fills in the detail, with the Indian law and the research behind it, so you can make the choice yourself. I hold an ICF coaching credential, so you should read what follows knowing where I sit. I've tried to be fair to the other three chairs.

What is the difference between a coach, a counsellor and a therapist?

The cleanest way to tell them apart is by the question each one is built to answer.

A coach answers "where am I going, and how do I get there?" The work assumes you're functioning: you can get up, go to work, think clearly on a good day. What's missing is direction, a decision, or a way of handling a role that has started to cost you. Executive coaching is simply this work done with senior people, often about leadership, transitions and the identity questions that come with them.

A counsellor answers "what is going on with me, and why does it hurt?" The work is slower and sits closer to feeling. Grief, loneliness, a marriage under strain, the flatness that arrives after a promotion you wanted. Counselling doesn't need a diagnosis to be useful, and most of the people who come for it don't have one.

A therapist, in India, usually means a clinical psychologist: someone trained to assess and treat mental illness using psychological methods. The word "therapist" itself has no legal meaning here, which is part of the confusion. Anyone can print it on a card.

A psychiatrist answers "is this an illness, and does it need medicine?" They are medical doctors who specialised in psychiatry after their MBBS. Many also do talking treatment, but what sets them apart is that they can diagnose, prescribe and, where necessary, admit.

Two things in that table deserve a closer look. The first is that the two most-searched labels, coach and counsellor, are the two with the least regulation behind them in India. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 defines psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, psychiatric social workers and mental health nurses, and asks each State Mental Health Authority to register the last three. It does not mention coaches or counsellors at all. The Rehabilitation Council of India maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register, and its list of registered categories includes clinical psychologists alongside rehabilitation and vocational counsellors for disability work, but no category for a general counsellor. So the title "counsellor" tells you very little on its own. What matters is the training behind it, and you should feel free to ask.

The second is what a coaching credential does and doesn't tell you. The ICF's PCC requirements are real: 125 hours of coach-specific education, 500 hours of coaching experience with at least 450 of them paid, 10 hours of mentor coaching, a recorded performance evaluation and a credentialing exam. It's a meaningful filter, and the directory is public. But it's a filter for coaching skill, not for clinical judgement. A PCC does not qualify anyone to tell you whether you have depression.

Can a coach help with anxiety or burnout?

It depends on which anxiety, and which burnout. There's a kind that is shaped like work: the knot that appears on Sunday evening and loosens on a Friday, the dread tied to a particular meeting, the flatness that lifts for a fortnight on holiday and then comes back. It's real, it costs you, and a coach can work with it well, because it usually sits on top of a decision you haven't made or a pattern you haven't named. I've written about this version in languishing and not being able to switch off.

Then there's the kind that has stopped responding to circumstances. It's there on holiday. It's there at 3 a.m. It has changed how you eat and sleep. Your partner has noticed. That version may be an anxiety disorder or a depressive episode, and a coach is the wrong first door, not because coaches are useless with it but because no one should be guessing. A clinical psychologist can assess it. A psychiatrist can tell you whether medicine would help. Coaching can follow once you're steadier, and often does.

Burnout follows the same split. If it's mostly exhaustion from a role that has outgrown you, coaching and counselling have plenty to offer. If it has tipped into something that looks like depression, you need the clinician first. If you aren't sure which side of that line you're on, that uncertainty is itself a reason to get an assessment. It costs an hour and removes the guessing.

When should you see a therapist or psychiatrist instead of a coach?

The honest answer is: sooner than most senior professionals do. The National Mental Health Survey of India 2015-16, run by NIMHANS across 12 states, found that 10.6% of adults were living with a mental disorder at the time of the survey, and that the treatment gap, the share of people with a condition who weren't getting care, ranged from 28% to 83% across disorders. In its own summary, nearly 150 million Indians needed mental health care and fewer than 30 million were seeking it. The survey also found the prevalence of mood disorders and stress-related disorders was two to three times higher in urban metros than elsewhere. Mumbai is one of those metros.

So the base rate matters. If you're a senior professional in a big Indian city, the odds that what you're carrying is a clinical condition rather than a career question are not negligible, and the default for most people is still to see nobody. Against that backdrop, the signals that should send you to a clinician first are simple:

  • Low mood, anxiety or dread on most days for several weeks, not just around specific events.
  • Sleep or appetite badly disrupted, or a marked change in energy that rest doesn't touch.
  • Any thoughts of harming yourself, or feeling that others would be better off without you.
  • Using alcohol or anything else to get through the day or the night.
  • Panic attacks, or experiences (hearing or seeing things) that others don't share.
  • A history of a diagnosed condition that feels like it's returning.

If any of those apply, book a psychiatrist or a clinical psychologist. If you're in crisis, don't book anyone. Call Tele-MANAS on 14416, the Government of India's free, round-the-clock mental health line, or go to the nearest emergency department.

Prescribing is the clearest line of all. Under the Mental Healthcare Act, a psychiatrist is a medical practitioner with a postgraduate qualification in psychiatry, and under the National Medical Commission Act, 2019 only someone enrolled on the State or National Register may practise medicine. No coach, counsellor or psychologist can write you a prescription, and anyone who suggests they can is telling you something important about themselves.

Does coaching actually work? What the evidence says

For a long time the honest answer was "probably, but the studies are weak". That changed in 2023. Erik de Haan and Viktor Nilsson published a meta-analysis in Academy of Management Learning & Education that admitted only randomised controlled trials, the design that medicine uses to decide whether a treatment works. It pooled 37 trials of workplace and executive coaching published between 1994 and 2021, covering 2,528 people in 39 samples.

The result was a statistically significant, moderate effect across leadership and personal outcomes, with a best estimate of g = 0.59. In plain terms, people who were coached ended up meaningfully better off than comparable people who weren't, on the things the trials measured. Three details matter for anyone deciding whether to spend money on it. The effect was larger when outcomes were self-reported than when someone else observed them, so part of what coaching produces is a change in how you see yourself. The authors found signs of publication bias, which usually means the true effect is somewhat smaller than the pooled number. And the effect didn't depend much on how long the coaching ran. Longer wasn't reliably better, which is why Clearhead's packages start at four sessions rather than forty.

Notice what the evidence doesn't say. These were trials of coaching for workplace and leadership outcomes in people who were, by and large, functioning. They are not evidence that coaching treats depression or anxiety disorders, and I wouldn't use them that way. For clinical conditions, the evidence base belongs to psychological treatment and psychiatry, and that is where you should go.

Coaching has good evidence for the problems it was designed for. The mistake is using it for the ones it wasn't.

Can you do both at once?

Yes, and it's more common than people expect. Someone on medication from a psychiatrist may still have a career decision to make, and the psychiatrist is not the right person for that conversation. Someone in treatment with a clinical psychologist for an anxiety disorder may want, in parallel, a place to think about what they actually want from the next five years. The two kinds of help don't compete. They answer different questions.

A few conditions make it work. Both professionals should know the other exists, with your permission, so nobody is working against the grain of the other. The clinical work takes precedence when it needs to; if your psychologist thinks coaching is adding pressure at the wrong moment, that opinion wins. And the coach should be clear about which chair they're sitting in. The ICF's Code of Ethics asks credentialed coaches to work within the boundaries of their competency and training, to disclose when they're acting in any other role, and to raise the possibility of a different professional or resource when the coaching relationship isn't delivering what the client needs. Those aren't formalities. They're what keeps the arrangement safe.

The order can also run the other way. People sometimes start with coaching because it feels less loaded, and a few sessions in it becomes clear that something heavier is underneath. A good coach names that and helps you find the clinician, rather than quietly trying to be one.

What to take from this

Choose by the question, not the label. Direction and decisions: a coach. Something that hurts and wants understanding: a counsellor. Symptoms that have lasted weeks or affect how you function: a clinical psychologist. Possible need for medication, or severe symptoms: a psychiatrist. Thoughts of harming yourself: 14416, now. And if you're not sure which of these you are, an assessment from a clinician is cheaper, in every sense, than a wrong guess.

Common questions about choosing between a coach, a counsellor and a therapist

Should I see a coach or a therapist?

Start from what is happening, not from the label. If the question is about direction, a decision, a role or how you are handling work, a coach fits. If something is weighing on you emotionally and you want to understand it, a counsellor fits. If low mood or anxiety has lasted weeks, is disrupting your sleep or daily functioning, or you have had thoughts of harming yourself, see a clinical psychologist or psychiatrist first. A good coach will tell you the same.

What is the difference between an executive coach and a therapist in India?

An executive coach works with a functioning professional on performance, decisions and how they lead and live. The title is not regulated by Indian law; the nearest standard is a voluntary ICF credential, which you can check in ICF's public Verify a Coach directory. A therapist, in the clinical sense, is usually a clinical psychologist, defined in the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 by an RCI-recognised qualification, who can assess and treat mental illness. Neither can prescribe medication; only a psychiatrist, who is a medical doctor, can.

Can a coach help with anxiety or burnout?

With the everyday, work-shaped kind, often yes: the pressure that lifts on holiday, the dread that is tied to a role or a decision. A 2023 meta-analysis of 37 randomised trials found a moderate overall effect of workplace coaching. But a coach does not diagnose or treat an anxiety disorder or clinical depression. If the anxiety is there most days for weeks, follows you home, or is affecting sleep, appetite or safety, that belongs with a clinical psychologist or psychiatrist, and coaching can come later.

Who can diagnose and prescribe for mental health in India?

Under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, a psychiatrist is a medical practitioner with a postgraduate degree or diploma in psychiatry, and under the National Medical Commission Act 2019 only someone on the State or National Register may practise medicine. So of the four roles compared here, only a psychiatrist can prescribe medication. Clinical psychologists assess and treat mental illness using psychological methods. Coaches and counsellors do neither.

What should I do if I am in crisis right now?

Do not book a coach or a counsellor. Call Tele-MANAS on 14416, the Government of India's free, round-the-clock mental health line, or go to the nearest emergency department. Coaching and counselling at Clearhead are not medical or emergency care.
