Five years, no promotion.
Why a career plateau quietly hollows you out.

On career plateaus, the frustration nobody admits to & the mentor who changes it · 9 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai · Updated 14 Sep 2026

Somewhere in the last year you did the arithmetic you had been avoiding, and the number came out at five. Five appraisal cycles with the same title on the door, five rounds of watching people you once trained walk into rooms you are no longer invited to, five years of being described as reliable in a voice that used to say promising. Nothing dramatic has happened to you, which is exactly why you have never said any of this out loud, because there is no story to tell, only a slow flattening that you carry into every Monday and quietly put down again every Friday.

If that lands somewhere tender, a study published three weeks ago followed 401 professionals through exactly this experience for four months and measured what a plateau does to a person from the inside, and then what it does to how that person behaves in a way their own manager could see. The findings are precise, and they are not flattering to the idea that you can simply wait this out. They also point to one specific thing that turned the whole pattern off, and it was a person, which is why this piece exists.

What is a career plateau, and which kind are you on?

In August 2026, Yufu Wang, Muhammad Zada and colleagues published a study in Frontiers in Psychology that followed 401 pairs of mentors and the professionals they mentored inside service-sector organisations, including banking and financial services, IT services, telecommunications and professional services firms. The professionals were on average nearly 34 years old and more than two thirds held a master's degree or higher, which is to say these were people who had done everything right on paper. The researchers measured the plateau in month one, job frustration two months later, and then, two months after that, asked each person's own mentor, who was also their direct supervisor, to rate how much the person had begun avoiding work.

The study separated two kinds of plateau, and the distinction matters because most people are only aware of the first one. A hierarchical plateau is the obvious version, the recognition that a higher title has become unlikely in your organisation, measured with statements such as being unlikely to obtain a much higher job title where you work. A job content plateau is the quieter version, the sense that the work itself has stopped stretching you and that your tasks will only become more routine from here. Both kinds, measured separately, predicted job frustration two months on, and both, separately, predicted the avoidance the supervisors later saw. You can be climbing and still be on the second kind, and you can be stuck on the ladder while the work is still alive, and the research suggests each one does its own damage.

How common is a mid-career stall?

The numbers say you have far more company than the silence around this topic suggests. In June 2026 the Burning Glass Institute and the NYU School of Professional Studies published a report called Sidetracked: The Hidden Crisis in Mid-Career Mobility, built on the career histories of 65 million US workers, and it found that 24.2 percent of mid-career professionals had gone five years or more with no promotion and no wage increase. That is one in four professionals who are still employed, still delivering, and going nowhere at all. Finance came in at 26.6 percent, and the stall tended to become visible around the ten-year mark of a career, which for most of the people who read this site is somewhere in their mid to late thirties.

The report found something more uncomfortable than the headline. By the ten-year mark, the people who eventually stalled had already fallen behind, averaging 1.5 promotions and 45 percent wage growth against 1.9 promotions and 66 percent for peers who kept moving. The stall was rarely a sudden event and was more often the last chapter of a slow-moving slump that had been building for years while everyone, including the person living it, called it stability. That is the part that hurts to read, because it means the flatness you noticed this year probably started long before you let yourself see it, and that the sense of running late that visits you at 2 a.m. is arithmetic you could have checked years ago.

Why does being stuck hurt capable people so much?

The Wang study used a framework called affective events theory, which holds that what happens at work reaches your behaviour by first passing through your feelings, and the feeling it identified was job frustration, the specific, grinding experience of trying to get something done and finding the effort blocked at every turn. For a person whose entire adult identity was built on competence, a plateau creates exactly that experience on a scale of years, because the thing you are trying to get done is your own life, and the block is invisible and nobody will name it. You keep performing the role well, which means there is nothing to fix, and the absence of anything to fix is precisely what makes the frustration so hard to metabolise.

Then there is the question of what the frustration turns into once it has nowhere left to go. The supervisors in the study were asked, four months after the plateau was first measured, how much the person avoided approaching the workplace when possible, how much they had begun to disengage and procrastinate, how much of their physical and mental presence had quietly been withdrawn. The plateaued professionals scored higher on all of it, and the frustration was the channel. This is the finding worth sitting with, because it means the withdrawal you think you are hiding behind a still-solid performance has a good chance of being visible to the person deciding your next promotion, which then makes the plateau steeper, which then deepens the withdrawal, a circle that tightens a little more with every appraisal cycle.

It also explains why the plateau rarely feels like a career problem from the inside. It feels like a flatness that arrived in your forties for no reason you can point to, or like being underused while still being busy, or like a boredom you are ashamed of at a job other people would want. Each of those is a real experience with its own research behind it, and each of them is often a plateau wearing a different coat.

Nobody stalls loudly, which is why nobody around you has noticed either. You just keep being good at the job while a part of you stops turning up, and the first person to notice is very often the one whose opinion decides whether you ever move again.

What turned the frustration off?

Here is the finding that should change what you do next. The study measured how proactive each professional believed their mentor to be, using statements like the mentor always looking for better ways to do things, and then tested whether that changed how a plateau turned into frustration. When the mentor was seen as passive or absent, the effect was severe, and a plateau produced sharply higher frustration. When the mentor was seen as proactive, someone who anticipated the obstacle and started conversations about it before being asked, the link between being plateaued and feeling frustrated fell to nothing and then reversed, so that plateaued professionals with a proactive mentor were, if anything, slightly less frustrated than their unplateaued peers. The authors are careful to say a plateau remains a bad thing in itself, and what their data shows is that the same stalled career, experienced with the right person alongside you, stops corroding you from the inside.

Read that against the Sidetracked report, which found that a sideways move into an adjacent role inside the same company, in the example it modelled, reduced the risk of stalling by as much as 86 percent. Put the two findings together and a clear picture emerges. The way out of a plateau usually runs sideways, and the people who find the sideways door are overwhelmingly the ones who had someone actively looking for it with them. The people who stay stuck are the ones who waited to be noticed, which is a strategy that feels dignified and is, on the evidence, the most expensive one available.

How do you tell whether this is a plateau or just a slow year?

The hard part is that a plateau is vague from the inside, and vague things cannot be worked on. It shows up as a reluctance you cannot justify, a Sunday-evening heaviness you would rather not admit to, an irritation with colleagues that has nothing to do with them. Giving the fog a shape is most of the relief, because once you can see which kind of plateau you are on, you can see which door is actually open.

Where a real conversation comes in

Notice what the two pieces of research are pointing at when you put them side by side. A plateau is common enough to be a quarter of your peers, it starts long before you admit it, it travels through frustration into a withdrawal that your manager can see, and the single most powerful thing that interrupted the whole sequence was a person who was actively thinking about your next move with you. Most senior professionals reading this do not have that person, because the higher you go, the fewer people are positioned to be proactive on your behalf, and the more your own pride insists that asking for one would be an admission of something.

That is a fair description of what good coaching is for. An hour with someone whose only job is to anticipate the obstacle with you, to ask the questions your manager has stopped asking, to help you tell a hierarchical plateau from a job content plateau, and to look for the sideways door with you before the withdrawal has cost you the option. Coaching will never hand you a promotion, and what it can do is stop a stalled career from corroding the person living it, which the evidence suggests is the part that decides whether you ever move again.

If you recognised the arithmetic in the first paragraph, and the silence you have kept around it, that recognition is worth taking seriously, and it is worth taking to a person rather than back to another appraisal cycle in the hope that this one will be different.

Common questions about career plateaus and feeling stuck

What is a career plateau?

A career plateau is the point where your career keeps running without rising. Researchers split it into two kinds. A hierarchical plateau is when a higher title or promotion has become unlikely in your organisation. A job content plateau is when the work itself has stopped stretching you and your tasks have turned routine. A 2026 study in Frontiers in Psychology measured both in 401 professionals and found that each one, on its own, predicted job frustration and then workplace avoidance that supervisors could see.

How common is a mid-career stall?

It is more common than most stalled people believe. The June 2026 Sidetracked report from the Burning Glass Institute and NYU School of Professional Studies analysed the career histories of 1.3 million stalled US mid-career professionals, drawn from a database of 65 million workers, and found that 24.2 percent of mid-career professionals had gone five years or more with no promotion and no wage increase. The stall typically becomes visible around the ten-year mark, and by then the people who stall have already averaged 1.5 promotions and 45 percent wage growth against 1.9 promotions and 66 percent for peers who keep moving.

Does a career plateau affect mental health?

The 2026 study measured frustration and avoidance, and it left clinical depression and anxiety to other work, so precision matters here. What it showed is the emotional route a plateau travels. Being plateaued at the first measurement predicted higher job frustration two months later, and that frustration predicted more workplace avoidance two months after that, including disengaging, procrastinating and staying away from the workplace when possible. Earlier research the paper builds on links plateaus to lower wellbeing, lower job satisfaction and stronger intentions to quit. In practice, a plateau tends to show up as a flatness and a quiet withdrawal that people hide behind a still-competent performance.

What actually helps when you feel stuck in your career?

The strongest finding in the 2026 study was about a single person. When professionals had a mentor they saw as proactive, someone who anticipated the obstacle and started conversations about it before being asked, the link between being plateaued and feeling frustrated fell to nothing and even reversed. With a passive or absent mentor, the same plateau produced sharply higher frustration. The Sidetracked report adds that a sideways move into an adjacent role inside the same company cut stall risk by up to 86 percent in the example it modelled. So the two things that help most are a person who will think about your next move with you, and a concrete view of which sideways moves are actually open to you.
