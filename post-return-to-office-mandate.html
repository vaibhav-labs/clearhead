You've been ordered back to the office.
So why does it feel like a loss?

On autonomy & mental health · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

It probably arrived as an email. A subject line that looked like any other policy update, and then a sentence that quietly rearranged your life. Back in the office, four or five days a week, starting next month. You read it twice, waiting for the part that asked whether it worked for you, or that acknowledged the two years you had spent proving the work got done from wherever you were. There was no such part. The decision had already been made, somewhere you were not in the room.

If being ordered back has left you more resentful, foggy, and drained than the extra commute alone can explain, that reaction is documented, not a character flaw. A 2026 study in the journal Public Personnel Management found that abrupt return-to-office mandates reduced employees' sense of control over their work and introduced real emotional strain. What was taken was your autonomy, not your comfort, and the mind registers that kind of loss for a long time.

And then the thought you did not type into the team channel, because everyone is in the same boat and saying it out loud feels small: this does not feel like a schedule change. It feels like something being taken back. You are not imagining that, and you are not being dramatic about it.

What being ordered back actually does to people

In early 2025, the United States government ended remote work for federal employees more or less overnight, which created a natural experiment that researchers moved quickly to study. In a 2026 paper in Public Personnel Management, Geneviève Morin, Carey Doberstein and Étienne Charbonneau analysed three waves of federal workforce surveys collected across 2024 and 2025 to see how people actually experienced the forced return.

Their finding is blunt. The mandate, in their words, disrupted established routines, reduced employees' sense of control over their work environment, and introduced new emotional and logistical strains. Reading the responses, they saw three things happening at once. There was an affective crisis, meaning a spike in frustration, anger and stress. There was a sense that performance was being made worse, not better, by the disruption. And there was a loss of control, the specific feeling of having your autonomy and your voice removed in the same stroke.

That middle part is worth pausing on, because it undercuts the usual justification. People were not, on the whole, reporting that they worked better back in the building. Many reported the opposite, that being ordered back made the work harder to do well. The mandate was sold as a productivity measure. It was often experienced as the thing getting in the way.

The office did not take your comfort. It took your say in your own day, and the mind keeps noticing that loss long after the commute stops feeling new.

Why losing the choice hurts more than the commute

It is tempting to explain the whole thing by the commute, because the commute is the visible cost. But the research points somewhere less obvious and more uncomfortable. The heaviest part is not the extra ninety minutes in traffic or on a packed train. It is the removal of control.

Occupational health has understood this for decades. The demand-control model, first set out by Robert Karasek in 1979, found that it is not high demand on its own that breaks people down. It is high demand paired with low control over how you meet it. A job can ask a great deal of you and still feel survivable when you get a say in how, when and where you do it. Take the say away and leave the demands untouched, and the same job starts to corrode the person doing it.

This is not a soft claim. A 2024 study of more than 5,000 workers found that persistent low job control more than doubled the risk of major depression, an effect worth reading in its own right here. A return mandate is, in the language of that research, a large and sudden cut to your control, applied to a workload that did not shrink to match. Your body reads that accurately. The flat mood, the shortened fuse, the Sunday-evening heaviness are not an overreaction. They are the expected response to a genuine change in your conditions.

It is not fragility. It is your best people doing the maths.

If some part of you feels quietly ashamed of how much this has affected you, here is the reframe worth sitting with. The people most likely to walk over a return mandate are not the disengaged ones at the edges. They are the strongest ones at the centre.

Researchers at the University of Pittsburgh tracked the LinkedIn work histories of more than three million employees at S&P 500 firms after return-to-office mandates took effect. Turnover rose sharply, and it rose most among senior, highly skilled, and women employees, which is to say the exact people a company can least afford to lose. Vacancies then took meaningfully longer to fill, roughly a quarter longer on average. The researchers gave the pattern a plain name: brain drain.

Read that as information about you, not only about companies. Your reaction is not a signal that you cannot cope with a normal ask. It is the same reaction the most capable, most mobile people in every one of those firms had at the same time. Feeling the pull toward the door is simply what competent, in-demand professionals feel when their autonomy is cut without a conversation. The real question is not whether you should be feeling this. It is what you decide to do with the feeling.

What actually helps when you cannot change the policy

You probably cannot reverse the mandate. Most people cannot. But there are three separate questions tangled inside the resentment, and pulling them apart is where relief actually begins.

The first is practical. What is this costing you, specifically? Not in vague dread, but in the real currency of a working mind: attention, recovery, the hours you had been spending on your family, your health, or the focused work you do best when nobody is around. A cost that stays vague stays frightening. That is genuinely hard to do alone, because you are too close to your own life to see which parts of it are load-bearing and which parts you have outgrown.

Common questions about return-to-office mandates and mental health

Why do return-to-office mandates feel so stressful?

Because the main thing they remove is not comfort, it is control. A 2026 study in Public Personnel Management analysed three waves of federal workforce surveys and found that abrupt return-to-office mandates disrupted established routines, reduced employees' sense of control over their work, and introduced new emotional and logistical strains. People reported frustration, anger and stress, and a feeling that their autonomy and their voice had been taken at once. The stress is a response to lost control, not a sign you cannot cope.

Is it normal to feel low, angry or depressed about returning to the office?

Yes, and there is a mechanism behind it. Occupational health research has long shown that high demands combined with low control are what wear people down, not demands alone. A 2024 study of more than 5,000 workers found that persistent low job control more than doubled the risk of major depression. A return mandate is a sudden cut to your control applied to a workload that did not shrink, so low mood and irritation are a predictable response to a real change in your conditions, not an overreaction.

Are return-to-office mandates actually making people quit?

The strongest people, most of all. Researchers at the University of Pittsburgh tracked the LinkedIn work histories of more than three million employees at S&P 500 firms and found that turnover rose sharply after return-to-office mandates, and rose most among senior, highly skilled, and women employees. Vacancies then took roughly a quarter longer to fill. The researchers called the pattern brain drain. Feeling the pull toward the door is what capable, in-demand professionals feel when their autonomy is cut.
