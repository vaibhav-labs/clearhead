You keep working harder.
What wears you down is how little you get to decide.

On control & mental health · 8 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

You have been told the same thing your whole career. If it feels heavy, work harder. If you are stretched, get more efficient. So you do. You answer faster, you carry more, you turn yourself into the person who can absorb anything. And still, somewhere in the last year or two, something went quietly flat. Not a crash, not a breakdown, just a slow draining of the color out of work you used to care about, and you assume it means you are not trying hard enough.

Here is what the research says, put plainly. The thing most likely to be wearing you down is not the size of the load. It is how little of it you actually get to decide. A large study that tracked working adults for years found that people stuck with persistently low control over their own work were about twice as likely to develop major depression as those who kept a real say in theirs. Not the busiest people. The ones with the least authority over how their days went. Your effort was never the problem. The lack of a hand on the wheel might be.

Can a lack of control at work really cause depression?

In 2024, a team led by Dr Yamna Taouk at the University of Melbourne published a study in the journal Social Science & Medicine using data on 5,054 employed men, followed from 2013 to 2021 as part of the Australian Longitudinal Study on Male Health. They measured how much control each man had over his own work, things like whether he could decide how tasks got done and had a say in the pace, and then watched who went on to develop symptoms of major depression.

The pattern was stepwise and hard to look away from. Compared with men who kept high control over their work, those with intermittently low control had about a 32 percent higher risk of later major depression. Those with persistently low control, the ones who stayed stuck without a say year after year, had roughly a 103 percent higher risk, more than double. Because control was measured before the depression showed up, this is not simply low people reporting low jobs. The low control came first. This is one cohort, and association is not the same as proof, but it points exactly where decades of workplace research already pointed.

Why does having no say hurt more than having too much to do?

This is not a new idea, it is one of the most durable findings in the study of work and health. Back in 1979, the sociologist Robert Karasek laid out what became known as the demand-control model. His point was simple and easy to miss. A demanding job is not what harms you. The dangerous combination is high demand plus low control, what he called a high-strain job. When you have to deliver a great deal but have little authority over how, when, or in what order it happens, the pressure has nowhere to go and no way to be shaped. Give the same person real control over the same load, and much of the damage disappears.

That is why two people in the same team, carrying the same workload, can end up in completely different places. One gets to decide the sequence, protect a block of focus, and say no to the third meeting. The other is available on demand, reacts all day, and owns results that depend on ten people who do not report to them. The hours look identical on paper. The strain does not.

A high-demand job does not break you on its own. It is high demand with no hand on the wheel that does the quiet damage, and a promotion can hand you more of the first while quietly taking away the second.

You're senior, so shouldn't you have plenty of control?

This is the part that catches capable people off guard. We assume seniority buys control, so if you feel powerless you must be imagining it. But promotions reliably add responsibility, and they do not reliably add authority. You climb, and suddenly you own outcomes shaped by budgets you did not set, timelines handed down from above, and stakeholders whose priorities keep changing under you. Your calendar fills with other people's decisions. You are accountable for everything and in charge of surprisingly little. That is responsibility without real control, the high-strain quadrant in an expensive suit.

The senior professionals I sit with rarely open by saying they lack control. They say they feel weirdly trapped in a role they worked years to get, that the work has become reactive, that they cannot remember the last decision that was fully theirs. When we trace the flatness back, it is almost never about the volume. It is about how much of their day is dictated by forces they cannot move. This sits close to two other patterns worth reading: the quiet cost of pouring in more than you get back, and the trap of a role that pays too well to leave and costs too much to stay in.

How common is this, really, among people like you?

Common enough that it has stopped registering as a problem. Across corporate India, low control usually arrives dressed as micromanagement, rigid mandates, and decisions that get made two levels above you and land on your desk as instructions. In workplace surveys, around 70 percent of employees say they have considered leaving a job because of a micromanaging boss, and roughly two thirds say it lowered their morale. When almost everyone around you is operating with a thin margin of control, having no say starts to feel like just how work is, rather than a measurable risk to your health. Naming it is the first move, because a norm is not the same as a safe level.

There is a heavier version of this too. Living for years with high demand and low control does not only lower today's mood, it keeps the body's stress system switched on, which is the thread running through a separate piece here on the wear-and-tear stress leaves on the body over time, and another on why the people holding everyone else together burn out first. Control is not a luxury. It is one of the few things that lets a demanding job stay survivable.

So what actually helps when you cannot just quit?

The honest answer is that most people in this spot do not need to try harder, they need to find the edges of their control and widen them, because even a thin margin has an outsized effect on strain. Start by separating what you genuinely cannot control from the smaller decisions that are quietly still yours, the order of your work, one protected block of focus, the meetings you could decline, the standard you set for what counts as done. Then look at the one or two decisions that should be yours and are not, and treat getting them back as real work, not a favor to ask for.

Where a real conversation comes in

Notice what the research is really pointing at. Not a discipline failure, and not a course to buy. A working life arranged so that you carry the weight but rarely hold the wheel, and a story you have been told for years, that any strain just means you should push harder, which keeps you doing the one thing that cannot fix a control problem. That story is hard to question alone, because the same drive that got you promoted is very good at defending the arrangement that is now draining you.

What tends to help is not another productivity tip. It is a real, two-way conversation with someone whose only job in that hour is to listen and help you think, so that the vague sense of being trapped becomes a clear map of what you actually control, what you could reclaim, and what is baked into the role and will not change no matter how hard you work. That is a fair description of what good coaching and counselling are for.

If you read this while feeling powerless in a job you worked hard to reach, that is worth taking seriously, and it is worth talking through with a person rather than pushing through for another year.

Common questions about job control and mental health

Can a lack of control at work actually cause depression?

The evidence points strongly that way. A 2024 study in Social Science & Medicine followed 5,054 employed Australian men from 2013 to 2021 and found that those with persistent low job control had roughly a 103 percent higher risk of later major depression symptoms than those with persistent high control. Men whose control dipped only some of the time still carried about a 32 percent higher risk. Because control was measured before the depression appeared, the direction runs from low control to low mood, not only the reverse.

Why does having no say hurt more than simply having too much work?

This is the core of the demand-control model set out by Robert Karasek in 1979. A demanding job is not the problem on its own. The most harmful combination is high demand paired with low control, what he called a high-strain job, because you carry the pressure without the authority to shape how the work gets done. Two people can face the same workload, and the one who can decide the how, the order, and the pace will fare far better than the one who cannot.

I am senior, so shouldn't I have plenty of control?

Not always, and this is the trap for many mid-career and senior professionals. Promotions reliably add responsibility, but they do not reliably add authority. You can end up owning outcomes you cannot fully influence, answerable to more stakeholders, with your calendar, priorities, and deadlines set by other people. That is responsibility without real control, which is exactly the high-strain quadrant the research warns about, even at a high salary.

What can you do if you cannot just quit the low-control job?

Start by separating the parts you genuinely cannot control from the smaller ones you can, because widening even a thin margin of control has an outsized effect on strain. Protect the decisions that are still yours, renegotiate one or two that should be, and get clear on whether the mismatch is fixable in this role or baked into it. A structured conversation with a coach or counsellor helps you see which is which before you burn out trying to fix the unfixable.
