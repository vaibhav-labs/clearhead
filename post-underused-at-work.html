You are not overworked.
You are underused.
And a part of you already knows it.

On being underused at work · 9 min read · Clearhead, Mumbai

On paper, the job is good. The pay is fine, the title reads well, the calendar is full enough that no one would call you idle. And yet, most days, you are running on maybe half of yourself. The problems that reach your desk are the ones you solved a decade ago. The hard, interesting parts of the work either never arrive or get handed elsewhere. You are competent, quick, dependable, and quietly, steadily bored in a way that has started to feel less like boredom and more like something going out.

Here is the part no one says out loud. You do not feel you have the right to be unhappy about it. People would trade places with you in a heartbeat. The salary is real, the stability is real, and so you file the restlessness under ingratitude and tell yourself to grow up. But the feeling does not go away just because you have decided it is not allowed. It sits there, low and constant, and it is doing more damage than you think. There is a name for what you are describing, there is fresh evidence behind it, and it is not a character flaw.

There is a name for it, and it is not laziness

Researchers call it skills-based underemployment. In plain language, it means working in a role that uses far less of your skill, experience and judgment than you actually have. It is the distance between what you are capable of and what your job asks of you on an ordinary Tuesday. Note that this is not the same as having too little to do. You can be busy all week and still be underused, because being busy and being stretched are two completely different things. One fills your hours. The other uses your mind.

For years this got waved away as a nice problem to have. Too much work is a crisis; too little of the right work is a luxury, or so the thinking went. That assumption is turning out to be wrong. When you are given a fraction of what you can carry, the unused part of you does not politely wait. It turns inward, and it starts to ask uncomfortable questions about whether any of this still means anything.

What the 2025 research actually found

In 2025, researchers publishing in Health Promotion International set out to test something that had mostly been assumed rather than measured: whether being underemployed actually shows up in a person's mental health. They drew on the HILDA survey, 21 straight years of data from 2002 to 2022, and followed 18,285 working-age Australians over time. Mental health was tracked with the MHI-5, a standard clinical scale. They looked at two kinds of underemployment side by side, having too few hours, and using too little of your skills.

Both mattered. Both forms of underemployment were linked to worse mental health over the years. And when they split the results by gender, one finding stood out: skills-based underuse, the sense of working below your capacity, had the strongest negative effect on men. This was not a quick snapshot that could be dismissed as a bad month. It was two decades of the same people tracked through their working lives, which is about as solid as this kind of evidence gets.

Being handed less than you can carry is not a soft complaint. In the research, it behaves like a chronic stressor, the same way too much pressure does, only quieter and easier to hide.

The researchers were careful to explain why. Drawing on what is called latent deprivation theory, they point out that a job is supposed to give you more than money. It is meant to give you a sense of purpose, a place in the world, a status and an identity. When your work uses only a sliver of you, it quietly withholds those things. You keep the paycheck and lose the meaning, and the loss of meaning is what your mind registers. That is why a well-paid, secure, respectable job can still leave a capable person feeling slowly hollowed out.

Why it hits the capable hardest

You might expect underuse to bother the ambitious least, since they can always chase something on the side. In practice it is the opposite. A 2018 study found that feeling overqualified turned into a real, corrosive sense of deprivation, and lower career satisfaction and wellbeing, most strongly in the people for whom their career mattered most. The more you have tied your identity to your work, the more it stings to spend your days on tasks that any competent person could do. Caring is not your protection here. Caring is the exposure.

Then there is the trap on top of the trap. Competence works as camouflage. The more capable you look, the less anyone imagines you might be struggling, so no one checks, no one reallocates the interesting work your way, and you are left to manage the quiet erosion alone. Add the belief that complaining about a good job is ungrateful, and you get a very specific kind of silence: a high performer coasting in plain sight, saying nothing, watching their own edge dull, and assuming they are the only one who feels this way.

They are not the only one. This is common enough that it has been measured across whole populations. What makes it feel so isolating is precisely that everyone hides it for the same reason, so no one ever hears that anyone else is carrying it too.

The gap has a shape, if you are willing to look at it

Underuse tends to live in the body as a vague, shapeless restlessness, a low static you cannot quite name. That vagueness is the worst part, because you cannot act on a fog. The moment you give it edges, the moment you can point to exactly where your capacity and your role have come apart, it stops being a verdict on your whole life and becomes a specific thing you can do something about.

What actually helps

The research does not just describe the problem. It quietly points at what softens it. Two things come up again and again.

The first is autonomy. A study of overqualified workers found that having genuine room to shape your own work, to stretch the role, take on more, and decide how things get done, buffered a lot of the harm that underuse otherwise causes. Often you cannot change the size of the job overnight, but you can widen it at the edges: reach for the harder problem, build the thing no one asked for, mentor the person who reminds you why you got good at this in the first place. Underuse shrinks when you stop waiting for permission to use yourself.

The second is having a real sense of a path. A 2026 study in Applied Psychology found that when people could see genuine opportunity ahead of them, the very same overqualification landed far more gently. The underuse hurts most when it feels permanent, like a room with no door. So it is worth getting honest about whether a door exists where you are. If part of the restlessness is a quiet question about whether you have outgrown this entirely, the Self-Employment Readiness Index is one calm way to test that question instead of just circling it at 2am.

And then there is the move the capable, self-reliant professional resists most, and needs most. Say it out loud to a real person. The story you have been telling yourself, that you have no right to feel this in a good job, that everyone else is fine, that wanting more from your work is greedy, almost never survives being spoken to someone who actually listens. It only holds power while it stays in your head. That is what a good coaching or counselling conversation is: not advice about your CV, but a real, unhurried space where the thing you have been quietly carrying finally gets named, looked at, and put in some kind of order. The waste is not that you feel underused. The waste is going years without ever saying so.

Common questions about being underused at work

What does it mean to be underused at work?

Being underused, or skills-based underemployment, means working in a role that draws on far less of your skill, experience and judgment than you actually have. It is not laziness or ingratitude. It is the gap between what you are capable of and what your role asks of you. A 2025 longitudinal study of 18,285 Australians in Health Promotion International found this kind of underemployment is tied to poorer mental health, and that skills-based underuse lands hardest on men.

Can being underused at work affect your mental health?

The evidence says yes. Using 21 years of data from 18,285 working-age Australians, measured with the MHI-5 scale, researchers found that both too few hours and too little use of your skills were linked to worse mental health over time. They explain it through relative deprivation and the loss of what a job is meant to provide beyond pay: purpose, status and identity.

Why do capable, senior people feel underused even in good jobs?

Because the people who feel it most sharply are often the ones who care most. A 2018 study found perceived overqualification turned into real deprivation, and lower wellbeing, most strongly in people for whom career mattered deeply. Competence also works as camouflage: the more capable you look, the less anyone imagines you are struggling, so you can coast in plain sight and say nothing, because complaining about a good job feels ungrateful.

What can you do if you feel underused at work?

Name the gap instead of carrying it as vague restlessness. The free Career Friction & Alignment Audit maps the distance between what you can do and what your role asks. Then lean on the two things research shows buffer it: autonomy, room to shape and stretch the work, and a real sense of a path forward. And say it out loud to someone, because underuse rarely resolves in silence. Coaching and counselling are built for exactly this.
